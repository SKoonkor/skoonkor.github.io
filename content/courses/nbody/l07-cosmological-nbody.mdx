## From a star cluster to a piece of the universe

The codes of Lectures 1&ndash;6 evolve isolated systems in a static space. To simulate the dark matter of the universe we change three things. **(i)** Space expands, so we use *comoving* coordinates that expand with it. **(ii)** The box is periodic and represents a fair sample of the universe, so we need the PM force of Lecture 6 and the periodic Poisson equation, with the mean density removed. **(iii)** The particles must start as a realisation of the early, nearly uniform universe, whose fluctuations are a Gaussian random field with a known power spectrum. We then analyse the result: the power spectrum and the halos.

<Callout kind="history" title="History">
Zel'dovich (1970) showed that the first non-linear structures are sheets ("pancakes"). Davis, Efstathiou, Frenk & White (1985) showed with periodic N-body simulations that cold dark matter reproduces galaxy clustering; Efstathiou et al. (1985) described the numerical techniques (PM, P<sup>3</sup>M) used for such boxes. Bardeen, Bond, Kaiser & Szalay (BBKS, 1986) gave the analytic transfer function used below. The Millennium simulation (Springel et al. 2005) followed <M t="2160^3\approx10^{10}" /> particles in a <M t="500\,h^{-1}" />Mpc box with a TreePM code; the box of this lecture has <M t="32^3" /> to <M t="64^3" /> particles.
</Callout>

## Gravity in an expanding universe

### Background expansion

A homogeneous, isotropic universe is described by the scale factor <M t="a(t)" /> (<M t="a=1" /> today). Its expansion rate <M t="H=\dot a/a" /> obeys the Friedmann equation, which for a flat universe with matter (density parameter <M t="\Omega_m" />) and a cosmological constant <M t="\Omega_\Lambda=1-\Omega_m" /> reads

<M d tag="1" id="eq-l07-hubble" t="H(a)=H_0\sqrt{\Omega_m a^{-3}+\Omega_\Lambda}." />

We use <M t="\Omega_m=0.3" />, <M t="\Omega_\Lambda=0.7" /> and units in which lengths are in <M t="\mathrm{Mpc}/h" /> and <M t="H_0=100" />, so velocities come out in <M t="\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}" /> and <M t="h" /> never appears. The special case <M t="\Omega_m=1" />, <M t="\Omega_\Lambda=0" /> is the *Einstein&ndash;de Sitter* (EdS) universe, <M t="H=H_0a^{-3/2}" />; it has simple analytic solutions, which we use as tests. It is convenient to use <M t="a" /> rather than time as the time variable: <M t="\dd t=\dd a/(aH)" />.

### Comoving coordinates

Let <M t="\vect r" /> be a physical position and <M t="\vect x=\vect r/a" /> the **comoving** position. A particle that moves only with the expansion has constant <M t="\vect x" />; its **peculiar velocity** <M t="\vect v=a\dot{\vect x}" /> is the motion relative to the expansion. The gravity that acts on the particle comes only from the density *contrast* <M t="\delta=\rho/\bar\rho-1" />, because the uniform part drives the background expansion. Write <M t="\vect r=a\vect x" />; then <M t="\ddot{\vect r}=a\ddot{\vect x}+2\dot a\dot{\vect x}+\ddot a\vect x" />. The last term is cancelled by the acceleration of the uniform background, <M t="\ddot a\vect x=-(4\pi G\bar\rho/3)\,a\vect x" /> (Friedmann's second equation for matter; a cosmological constant adds a term that cancels in the same way), and what remains of the gravitational force is that of the density contrast. Dividing by <M t="a" /> and using <M t="\nabla_r=a^{-1}\nabla_x" /> gives

<M d tag="2" id="eq-l07-eom" t="\ddot{\vect x}+2H\dot{\vect x}=-\frac{1}{a^2}\nabla_x\phi,\qquad\nabla_x^2\phi=4\pi G\,a^2\bar\rho\,\delta ." />

The term <M t="2H\dot{\vect x}" /> is Hubble friction (<M t="\nabla_x" /> is the gradient with respect to the comoving coordinate; <M t="\vect x" /> is in <M t="\mathrm{Mpc}/h" />). Because <M t="\bar\rho=3\Omega_mH_0^2/(8\pi G\,a^3)" />, the source of the potential is <M t="4\pi Ga^2\bar\rho\,\delta=\tfrac32\Omega_mH_0^2\,\delta/a" />. Now define the **canonical momentum** <M t="\vect p=a^2\dot{\vect x}" /> (so <M t="\vect v=\vect p/a" />; it is the momentum conjugate to <M t="\vect x" /> for the Lagrangian <M t="a^2\dot{\vect x}^2/2-\phi" />). Then <M t="\dot{\vect p}=a^2(\ddot{\vect x}+2H\dot{\vect x})=-\nabla_x\phi" />, which removes the friction term. Finally write <M t="\phi=\tilde\phi/a" />, so that <M t="\nabla^2\tilde\phi=a\nabla^2\phi" /> has a constant source:

<M d tag="3" id="eq-l07-canon" t="\frac{\dd\vect x}{\dd t}=\frac{\vect p}{a^2},\qquad\frac{\dd\vect p}{\dd t}=-\frac{1}{a}\nabla_x\tilde\phi,\qquad\nabla_x^2\tilde\phi=\tfrac32\,\Omega_mH_0^2\,\delta ." />

Two things have happened: the Hubble friction has disappeared from the equation of motion, and the source of the Poisson equation depends on time only through <M t="\delta" />. Your PM code from Lecture 6 already solves <M t="\nabla^2\tilde\phi=4\pi G\rho" /> with the mean density removed; we only have to choose the constants (see the section on running the box).

### Leapfrog in the expanding background

Equations (3) look like the Hamiltonian equations of Lecture 2 with two changes: the "drift" acts over <M t="\dd t/a^2" /> and the "kick" over <M t="\dd t/a" />. Since <M t="\vect g" /> depends only on positions, integrating <M t="\dd\vect p/\dd t=\vect g/a" /> over a half step with <M t="\vect g" /> held fixed gives <M t="\vect g\int\dd t/a" />, and likewise for the drift. The symplectic KDK leapfrog therefore carries over unchanged, with <M t="\Delta t" /> replaced by the two integrals below (use <M t="\dd t=\dd a/(aH)" /> to write them as integrals over <M t="a" />):

<M d tag="4" id="eq-l07-factors" t="I_{\rm kick}(a_0,a_1)=\int\frac{\dd t}{a}=\int_{a_0}^{a_1}\frac{\dd a}{a^2H(a)},\qquad I_{\rm drift}(a_0,a_1)=\int\frac{\dd t}{a^2}=\int_{a_0}^{a_1}\frac{\dd a}{a^3H(a)}." />

For a step from <M t="a_0" /> to <M t="a_1" /> with <M t="a_{1/2}=\sqrt{a_0a_1}" /> (or any midpoint):

<M d t="\vect p\leftarrow\vect p+\vect g(\vect x)\,I_{\rm kick}(a_0,a_{1/2}),\qquad\vect x\leftarrow\vect x+\vect p\,I_{\rm drift}(a_0,a_1),\qquad\vect p\leftarrow\vect p+\vect g(\vect x)\,I_{\rm kick}(a_{1/2},a_1)," />

where <M t="\vect g=-\nabla\tilde\phi" /> is the PM acceleration for the density contrast (Eq. (3)). The integrals are evaluated numerically (or analytically in EdS). A good choice of steps is uniform in <M t="\ln a" />, because the dynamical time grows with <M t="a" />.

```python title="s1_factors_eds.py"
import numpy as np

# Einstein-de Sitter: H(a) = H0 a^(-3/2). Kick factor = int da / (a^2 H).
H0, a0, a1 = 100.0, 0.2, 0.6
a = np.linspace(a0, a1, 2001)                  # fine grid in a
kick_num = np.trapezoid(1.0 / (a**2 * H0 * a**-1.5), a)
kick_exact = 2 * (np.sqrt(a1) - np.sqrt(a0)) / H0
print(kick_num, kick_exact)                    # 0.006547... twice
```

The listing checks the kick factor in an Einstein&ndash;de Sitter universe numerically.

<Callout kind="pitfall">
Using <M t="\dd t" /> instead of the kick and drift factors, or <M t="1/a" /> where <M t="1/a^2" /> belongs, produces a code that still runs and produces plausible-looking structure with the wrong growth rate. The pancake test in the Zel'dovich section below catches this.
</Callout>

## Linear growth of structure

While <M t="\delta\ll1" /> the density contrast of matter satisfies <M t="\ddot\delta+2H\dot\delta=\tfrac32\Omega_m(a)H^2\delta" />, with <M t="\Omega_m(a)=\Omega_ma^{-3}H_0^2/H^2" />. In the variable <M t="\ln a" /> this reads <M t="\delta''+\big(2+\tfrac{\dd\ln H}{\dd\ln a}\big)\delta'=\tfrac32\Omega_m(a)\,\delta" /> (primes: <M t="\dd/\dd\ln a" />), which can be integrated numerically. It has a growing and a decaying solution; the growing one, <M t="\delta(\vect x,a)=D(a)\,\delta_0(\vect x)" />, depends on time only through the **growth factor** <M t="D(a)" />, normalised here to <M t="D(1)=1" />. For a flat universe,

<M d tag="5" id="eq-l07-growth" t="D(a)\propto H(a)\int_0^a\frac{\dd a'}{[a'H(a')]^3},\qquad f(a)\equiv\frac{\dd\ln D}{\dd\ln a}\approx\Omega_m(a)^{0.55}." />

In EdS this gives <M t="D=a" /> and <M t="f=1" />. With a cosmological constant growth slows at late times (Figure 2). The rate <M t="f" /> enters the initial velocities. Checking your <M t="D(a)" /> against a numerical solution of this differential equation is an optional exercise in Homework 7.1 (the checker does it for you).

## Initial conditions

### The power spectrum

The early universe is described statistically: we cannot predict where each fluctuation is, only how strong the fluctuations are on each scale. The **power spectrum** <M t="P(k)" /> is the variance of the Fourier mode at wavenumber <M t="k" /> (per unit volume), and <M t="\sigma^2=\int P(k)\,\dd^3k/(2\pi)^3" /> is the variance of <M t="\delta" />. For a homogeneous, isotropic Gaussian field the Fourier modes <M t="\tilde\delta(\vect k)" /> are independent Gaussian variables with

<M d tag="6" id="eq-l07-pk" t="\big\langle\tilde\delta(\vect k)\tilde\delta^*(\vect k')\big\rangle=(2\pi)^3\delta_D(\vect k-\vect k')\,P(k)." />

On a periodic grid of volume <M t="V=L^3" /> with <M t="N_g=n^3" /> cells this becomes (with the unnormalised FFT of Lecture 6) <M t="\langle|\hat\delta_{\vect m}|^2\rangle=N_g^2\,P(k)/V" /> (the continuum relation, with <M t="(2\pi)^3\delta_D(0)\to V" />, gives <M t="\langle|\tilde\delta|^2\rangle=VP" />, and <M t="\hat\delta_{\vect m}=N_g\tilde\delta/V" /> for the unnormalised discrete transform). This gives two recipes:

- **Estimating <M t="P(k)" />:** <M t="P(k)=\dfrac{V}{N_g^2}\big\langle|\hat\delta_{\vect m}|^2\big\rangle" />, averaged over the modes with <M t="|\vect k|" /> in a shell around <M t="k" />. Use shells centred on multiples of the fundamental frequency <M t="k_f=2\pi/L" />: shell <M t="m" /> contains the modes with <M t="(m-\frac12)k_f\le|\vect k|<(m+\frac12)k_f" />, for <M t="m=1,\dots,n/2" />. The binning listing below shows the binning in 2D.
- **Generating a field:** draw a real white-noise field <M t="w(\vect x)" /> of unit variance per cell; its FFT has <M t="\langle|\hat w|^2\rangle=\sum_x\langle w^2\rangle=N_g" />, and because <M t="w" /> is real, <M t="\hat w(-\vect k)=\hat w^*(\vect k)" /> (Lecture 6), so after multiplying by a function of <M t="|\vect k|" /> the inverse transform is real again. (Filling Fourier space with random complex numbers yourself would require imposing this symmetry by hand.) Multiply <M t="\hat w" /> by <M t="\sqrt{N_g\,P(k)/V}" /> (zero the <M t="k=0" /> mode) and transform back; the result is a real field with power spectrum <M t="P(k)" />. The Gaussian-field listing below does this in 1D.

```python title="s4_shells_2d.py"
import numpy as np

n, L = 8, 8.0
k1 = 2 * np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(n, d=L / n)
kx, ky = np.meshgrid(k1, k1, indexing="ij")
kmag = np.hypot(kx, ky)
kf = 2 * np.pi / L                             # fundamental frequency
shell = np.rint(kmag / kf).astype(int)         # shell index 0, 1, 2, ...
power = np.ones((n, n))                        # pretend |delta_k|^2 = 1
counts = np.bincount(shell.ravel())
means = np.bincount(shell.ravel(), weights=power.ravel()) / counts
print(counts[:4], means[:4])                   
```

The listing bins <M t="|\hat\delta|^2" /> in shells (a 2D toy).

```python title="s2_gaussian_1d.py"
import numpy as np

n, L = 4096, 100.0
pk = lambda k: 50.0 * np.exp(-k)              # any positive toy spectrum
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
w = rng.standard_normal(n)                    # white noise: real, <|w_k|^2> = n
k = 2 * np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(n, d=L / n)
amp = np.sqrt(pk(np.abs(k)) * n / L)          # sqrt(P n / L) in 1D (n^3/V in 3D)
amp[0] = 0.0                                  # zero mean
delta = np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(w) * amp)
print(np.abs(delta.imag).max() < 1e-12)       # True: the field is real
print(np.var(delta.real), np.sum(pk(np.abs(k[1:]))) / L)   # similar (one realisation)
```

The listing generates a Gaussian random field with a given spectrum (a 1D toy).

For <M t="\Lambda" />CDM we use the BBKS fit <M t="P(k)\propto k^{n_s}T^2(k)" /> with <M t="T(q)=\frac{\ln(1+2.34q)}{2.34q}\big[1+3.89q+(16.1q)^2+(5.46q)^3+(6.71q)^4\big]^{-1/4}" />, <M t="q=k/(\Omega_mh)" />, <M t="n_s=0.96" />, <M t="h=0.7" />, normalised so that the linear rms fluctuation in spheres of radius <M t="8\,h^{-1}" />Mpc is <M t="\sigma_8=0.8" /> at <M t="z=0" />. <M t="\sigma_8" /> is <M t="\sigma" /> of <M t="\delta" /> smoothed with a spherical top-hat of radius <M t="8\,h^{-1}" />Mpc. The skeleton provides the spectrum as `bbks_pk` (it is a fit, not something to derive). The <M t="z=0" /> spectrum is scaled back to the initial time by <M t="D^2(a)" />.

### The Zel'dovich approximation

The particles start on a regular lattice at positions <M t="\vect q" /> (**Lagrangian** coordinates). Zel'dovich's approximation moves each particle along a straight line, with the displacement growing as <M t="D(a)" />:

<M d tag="7" id="eq-l07-zel" t="\vect x(\vect q,a)=\vect q+D(a)\,\vect\psi(\vect q),\qquad\vect\psi_{\vect k}=\frac{i\vect k}{k^2}\,\delta_{0,\vect k}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad-\nabla\!\cdot\!\vect\psi=\delta_0 ." />

Lagrangian coordinates <M t="\vect q" /> label particles; Eulerian coordinates <M t="\vect x" /> are positions in space. The condition <M t="-\nabla\cdot\vect\psi=\delta_0" /> follows from mass conservation: the mass <M t="\bar\rho\,\dd^3q" /> of a lattice cell ends up in the volume <M t="\dd^3x=\det(\delta_{ij}+D\,\partial\psi_i/\partial q_j)\,\dd^3q" />, so <M t="1+\delta=1/\det(\dots)\approx1-D\nabla\cdot\vect\psi" /> to first order, i.e. <M t="\delta=D\delta_0" /> with <M t="\delta_0=-\nabla\cdot\vect\psi" />. We take <M t="\vect\psi" /> curl-free (gravity is a gradient force, so the growing mode has no vorticity), which gives <M t="\vect\psi_{\vect k}\propto\vect k" />. The trajectory <M t="\vect x=\vect q+D\vect\psi" /> satisfies the equation of motion to first order because <M t="D" /> solves the growth equation. The momentum follows from <M t="\vect p=a^2\dot{\vect x}=a^2\dot D\,\vect\psi=a^2H\,f\,D\,\vect\psi" />. With <M t="\vect\psi" /> computed from <M t="\delta_0" /> by an FFT, a multiplication by <M t="i\vect k/k^2" /> (`np.fft` uses <M t="e^{-i\vect k\cdot\vect x}" /> in the forward transform, so this sign is right in the convention of Lecture 6) and an inverse FFT (zero the <M t="k=0" /> and Nyquist planes), this defines the initial conditions at a starting scale factor <M t="a_i" />, for example <M t="a_i=0.02" /> (<M t="z=49" />).

The Zel'dovich solution is exact for a plane wave, <M t="\delta_0=A\cos(kx)" />, until particles cross: then <M t="x=q-DA\sin(kq)/k" />, <M t="\dd x/\dd q=1-DA\cos(kq)" /> and the density <M t="\bar\rho/|\dd x/\dd q|" /> (before crossing) diverges when <M t="DA=1" />, first at <M t="q=0" /> and <M t="q=L" />. Neighbouring particles cross, forming a **caustic**, the sheet of a "pancake". Beyond that the approximation fails, but N-body gravity continues correctly. This gives a clean test of the whole machinery: run your code on a plane wave in an Einstein&ndash;de Sitter universe and compare with the analytic trajectories (Homework 7.4).

```python title="s3_zeldovich_1d.py"
import numpy as np

L, n, A = 100.0, 10, 1.3                       # box, particles, amplitude A = D * delta_0
k = 2 * np.pi / L
q = np.arange(n) * L / n                       # lattice (Lagrangian) positions
psi = -np.sin(k * q) / k                       # for delta_0 = cos(k q)
x = (q + A * psi) % L                          # Eulerian positions
print(np.round(x, 1))
dxdq = 1 - A * np.cos(k * q)                 # stretching dx/dq; density = 1 / dxdq
print(np.round(dxdq, 2))
print("shell crossing:", dxdq.min() <= 0)  # True for A = 1.3, False for A < 1
```

<Callout kind="pitfall">
Zel'dovich initial conditions carry errors of order <M t="D(a_i)" />: start too late and the transient decaying modes and second-order effects contaminate the result; start too early and the particle displacements are smaller than the numerical noise of the force. <M t="a_i=0.02" /> is adequate for our purposes; production codes use second-order Lagrangian perturbation theory (2LPT) and start around <M t="z\approx50" />&ndash;<M t="100" />.
</Callout>

## Running the box

Put everything together:

1. Draw <M t="\delta_0" /> from BBKS with `gaussian_field`; compute <M t="\vect\psi" />; build the lattice at <M t="a_i" /> with positions and momenta (`zeldovich_ics`).
2. For each step of the KDK scheme call the PM force of Lecture 6 on the current positions, with <M t="N" /> particles of unit mass, and <M t="G=3\Omega_mH_0^2L^3/(8\pi N)" />. Then the mean density is <M t="\bar\rho=N/L^3" /> and <M t="4\pi G\bar\rho=\tfrac32\Omega_mH_0^2" />, so `pm_acceleration` returns <M t="-\nabla\tilde\phi" /> of Eq. (3). Use CIC and the four-point gradient. The force is not divided by <M t="a" />; that is in the kick factor.
3. Advance in <M t="\ln a" /> from <M t="a_i" /> to <M t="1" />, storing snapshots at chosen <M t="a" />.

<Callout kind="idea">
The comoving PM code differs from the Lecture 6 code in three places only: the constants in <M t="G" /> (a product of <M t="\Omega_m" />, <M t="H_0" /> and the box size), the kick and drift factors instead of <M t="\Delta t" />, and <M t="\vect p=a^2\dot{\vect x}" /> instead of <M t="\vect v" />. All of it is contained in `evolve`.
</Callout>

### What to check: growth of the power spectrum

Linear theory says <M t="P(k,a)=D^2(a)P_0(k)" />. Measuring <M t="P(k)" /> of the particles requires the CIC density <M t="\rho" /> of the particles (Lecture 6), <M t="\delta=\rho/\bar\rho-1" />, division of <M t="\hat\delta" /> by the CIC window <M t="\prod_a\mathrm{sinc}^2(k_ah/2)" /> (the assignment smoothed the field; Lecture 6, Fig. 3), and the shell average. Do *not* subtract the shot noise <M t="V/N" /> here: it is the right correction for randomly placed particles, but a lattice (the initial conditions) has no shot noise, and the correction would give negative powers. At low <M t="k" /> the signal is well above <M t="V/N" /> from <M t="a\gtrsim0.1" /> anyway.

Three cautions apply to every P(k) of this kind. Below <M t="\sim2" />&ndash;<M t="3\,k_f" /> there are too few modes for a precise comparison. ## Finding halos: friends-of-friends

Dark matter collapses into gravitationally bound clumps, **halos**, in which galaxies form. The simplest halo finder is **friends-of-friends** (FoF; Davis et al. 1985): two particles are *friends* if their distance is less than a linking length <M t="b\,\bar d" />, where <M t="\bar d=L/N^{1/3}" /> is the mean particle spacing and <M t="b\simeq0.2" />; a halo is a connected group of friends (friends of friends are friends). The value <M t="b\simeq0.2" /> is the customary choice: it picks out roughly virialised objects.

An algorithm has two steps. **(1)** Find all pairs closer than <M t="b\bar d" /> without looking at all <M t="N^2" /> pairs: sort the particles into cells of side <M t="\ge b\bar d" /> (the *chaining mesh*, the same idea as in P<sup>3</sup>M of Lecture 8), and compare only particles in the same or neighbouring cells, using the periodic (minimum-image) distance. **(2)** Group particles connected by pairs: the connected-components listing below propagates the smallest label along the pairs until nothing changes. You should vectorise this over pairs with NumPy: for <M t="N=32^3" /> particles the reference finds the groups in about <M t="0.1" /> s.

```python title="s5_components.py"
import numpy as np

# Linked pairs 0-1, 1-2, 3-4 among 6 particles: groups {0,1,2}, {3,4}, {5}.
pairs = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (3, 4)]
label = np.arange(6)
changed = True
while changed:                                 # pass the smallest label along links
    changed = False
    for i, j in pairs:
        m = min(label[i], label[j])
        if label[i] != m or label[j] != m:
            label[i] = label[j] = m
            changed = True
print(label)                                   # [0 0 0 3 3 5]
```

The listing finds connected components by propagating the smallest label (a toy with 6 particles).

The halo mass function is described analytically by Press&ndash;Schechter (1974) and Sheth&ndash;Tormen (1999).

## Summary

- In comoving coordinates with <M t="\vect p=a^2\dot{\vect x}" /> the equations of motion are those of Lecture 2 with kick factor <M t="\int\dd a/(a^2H)" /> and drift factor <M t="\int\dd a/(a^3H)" />, and the force sourced by the density contrast, <M t="\nabla^2\tilde\phi=\tfrac32\Omega_mH_0^2\delta" />.
- Linear perturbations grow as <M t="D(a)" /> (Eq. (5)); velocities in the initial conditions carry <M t="f=\dd\ln D/\dd\ln a" />.
- A Gaussian field with spectrum <M t="P(k)" /> is white noise multiplied by <M t="\sqrt{N_gP(k)/V}" /> in Fourier space; the same normalisation measures <M t="P(k)" />.
- Zel'dovich initial conditions displace a lattice by <M t="D\,\vect\psi" /> with <M t="-\nabla\cdot\vect\psi=\delta_0" />; the plane-wave pancake is an exact test for the code until shell crossing.
- The PM run reproduces linear growth on large scales, and the cosmic web and non-linear power on small ones, and FoF finds the halos.
