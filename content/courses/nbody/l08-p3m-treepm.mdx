## Two methods, two weaknesses

The particle&ndash;mesh method of Lecture 6 is fast, periodic and independent of clustering, but its force is smoothed on the scale of a grid cell: for two particles closer than about <M t="2h" /> the force is wrong by tens of per cent, and inside a dense halo it is wrong by a factor of three or more (see the section on the cosmological run below). Tree and direct methods (Lectures 1&ndash;5) resolve any scale but have to sum over all far-away particles and do not treat periodicity naturally. Each method is good exactly where the other is poor. A **hybrid** method splits the force into two pieces, one for each:

<M d tag="1" id="eq-l08-split" t="\vect F(r)=\vect F_{\rm long}(r)+\vect F_{\rm short}(r)," />

where <M t="\vect F_{\rm long}" /> is smooth and is computed by the mesh, and <M t="\vect F_{\rm short}" /> is significant only at <M t="r\lesssim r_{\rm cut}" /> and is computed by summing over close neighbours: with a chaining mesh (**P<sup>3</sup>M**: particle&ndash;particle particle&ndash;mesh) or with a tree (**TreePM**). Both are used in current cosmological codes.

<Callout kind="history" title="History">
Hockney & Eastwood (1981) introduced P<sup>3</sup>M for plasmas and Efstathiou & Eastwood (1981) applied it to gravity. Couchman (1991) added adaptive refinement of the short-range sums in clustered regions (AP3M). Xu (1995) and Bagla (2002) combined a mesh with a tree (TreePM). GADGET-2 (Springel 2005) uses TreePM with the Gaussian split of this lecture, and so do its successors (GADGET-4, Springel et al. 2021, and AREPO for gravity); HACC and others use related splits.
</Callout>

## The Gaussian force split

We want a split of the Newtonian point-mass force <M t="Gm/r^2" /> such that <M t="\vect F_{\rm long}" /> is easy for a mesh (smooth, so its Fourier transform decays quickly) and <M t="\vect F_{\rm short}" /> vanishes beyond a finite radius. Split the *mass*, not the force: replace the point mass <M t="m" /> by a Gaussian cloud

<M d tag="2" id="eq-l08-cloud" t="\rho_s(r)=\frac{m}{(4\pi r_s^2)^{3/2}}\,e^{-r^2/4r_s^2},\qquad\hat\rho_s(k)=m\,e^{-k^2r_s^2}." />

The potential of this cloud follows from its enclosed mass <M t="M(<r)=m\,[\mathrm{erf}(r/2r_s)-\tfrac{r}{r_s\sqrt\pi}e^{-r^2/4r_s^2}]" /> (Gauss's law) or, in Fourier space, it is just the usual <M t="-4\pi Gm/k^2" /> times the filter <M t="e^{-k^2r_s^2}" />; in real space <M t="\Phi_{\rm long}=-Gm\,\mathrm{erf}(r/2r_s)/r" />. The short-range part is what is left of the point mass, the point mass *minus* the cloud:

<M d tag="3" id="eq-l08-potsplit" t="\frac{1}{r}=\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{erf}(r/2r_s)}{r}}_{\text{long range (mesh)}}+\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(r/2r_s)}{r}}_{\text{short range}} ." />

Differentiating the short-range term (with <M t="x=r/2r_s" /> and <M t="\dd\,\mathrm{erfc}(x)/\dd x=-\tfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-x^2}" />) gives its force,

<M d tag="4" id="eq-l08-S" t="\vect F_{\rm short}=\frac{Gm\,\hat{\vect r}}{r^2}\,S(r),\qquad S(r)=\mathrm{erfc}\!\Big(\frac{r}{2r_s}\Big)+\frac{r}{r_s\sqrt\pi}\,e^{-r^2/4r_s^2}," />

and the long-range force is <M t="Gm/r^2\,(1-S)" />; both are attractive, with <M t="\hat{\vect r}" /> the unit vector towards the source. (For <M t="r\ll r_s" />, <M t="S\to1" />: close pairs feel the full Newtonian force from the short-range part.) The function <M t="S" /> falls from <M t="1" /> at <M t="r=0" /> to <M t="0.5" /> at <M t="r\approx2.2\,r_s" /> and to <M t="0.0175" /> at <M t="4.5\,r_s" /> (Figure 1); the short-range force is dropped beyond a **cutoff** <M t="r_{\rm cut}=4.5\,r_s" />. The scale <M t="r_s" /> is a choice: GADGET-2 uses <M t="r_s=1.25\,h" />, where <M t="h" /> is the mesh cell size.

**Relation to the Ewald sum.** This is the same idea as the Ewald sum of Lecture 5, whose <M t="\mathrm{erfc}(\alpha r)" /> real-space and <M t="\mathrm{erf}" /> Fourier-space pieces correspond to <M t="\alpha=1/(2r_s)" />. The differences are in what is kept: Ewald sums the short-range piece over all periodic images and the long-range piece over many Fourier modes, to machine accuracy; P<sup>3</sup>M keeps only the nearest image within <M t="r_{\rm cut}" /> (fine, since <M t="r_{\rm cut}<L/2" /> and <M t="S" /> is small beyond) and the long-range piece on the grid. The mesh force is periodic automatically, and the <M t="\vect k=0" /> mode is dropped as in Lecture 6. This is also why the Ewald force is the right reference for testing.

**What to implement.** The function <M t="S" /> needs <M t="\mathrm{erfc}" />, which NumPy does not have. Use `math.erfc` through `np.frompyfunc`, or tabulate <M t="S" /> once and interpolate with `np.interp` (see the lookup-table listing below); both are fast enough here.

```python title="s1_table.py"
import math
import numpy as np

rs = 1.0
erfc = np.frompyfunc(math.erfc, 1, 1)          # numpy has no erfc: apply math.erfc element-wise
S = lambda r: erfc(r / (2 * rs)).astype(float) + r / (rs * math.sqrt(math.pi)) * np.exp(-(r / (2 * rs)) ** 2)

r_tab = np.linspace(0.0, 5.0, 501)             # tabulate once ...
S_tab = S(r_tab)
r = np.random.default_rng(0).random(5) * 4.5
print(np.abs(np.interp(r, r_tab, S_tab) - S(r)).max())   # ... look up with np.interp: error ~4e-6
```

The listing computes the short-range factor by table lookup (`np.frompyfunc` applies `math.erfc` element-wise).

## The long-range part on the mesh

The mesh needs only two changes to the PM code of Lecture 6. **(i)** Multiply <M t="\hat\rho(\vect k)" /> by <M t="e^{-k^2r_s^2}" /> before solving Poisson's equation: this is the Fourier-space version of Eq. (3). The filter listing below shows that <M t="e^{-k^2r_s^2}" /> smooths a point mass into a Gaussian of width <M t="\sqrt2\,r_s" />. **(ii)** Deconvolve the assignment window.

```python title="s2_gaussian_filter_1d.py"
import numpy as np

n, L, rs = 512, 1.0, 0.02
x = np.arange(n) * L / n
rho = np.zeros(n)
rho[n // 2] = n / L                            # a unit mass in one cell (delta function)
k = 2 * np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(n, d=L / n)
rho_s = np.fft.ifft(np.fft.fft(rho) * np.exp(-k**2 * rs**2)).real
dx = x - x[n // 2]
sigma = np.sqrt(np.sum(rho_s * dx**2) * L / n)  # width of the smoothed mass
print(sigma / rs)                              # ~1.414: exp(-k^2 rs^2) is a Gaussian of width sqrt(2) rs
```

The listing shows that the filter <M t="e^{-k^2r_s^2}" /> turns a point mass into a Gaussian cloud (a 1D toy).

### Why the window must be deconvolved

Mass assignment and force interpolation each convolve with the assignment kernel, whose Fourier transform is <M t="W(\vect k)=\prod_a\mathrm{sinc}^p(k_ah/2)" /> (<M t="p=2" /> for CIC, <M t="3" /> for TSC; Lecture 6). The mesh force is therefore smoothed by <M t="W^2" /> (once for assignment, once for interpolation) on top of the Gaussian filter, which makes it too small at <M t="r\lesssim3h" />, exactly where the short-range part hands over to it. Plain PM (Lecture 6) does not divide by <M t="W^2" /> because near the Nyquist wavenumber <M t="W^2" /> is small (<M t="0.16" /> for CIC and <M t="0.07" /> for TSC per axis at <M t="k_N=\pi/h" />) and the division would amplify grid noise and aliasing. Here the Gaussian filter has already suppressed those modes: for <M t="r_s=1.25h" />, <M t="e^{-k^2r_s^2}\approx2\times10^{-7}" /> at <M t="k_N" /> and <M t="0.02" /> at <M t="k_N/2" /> (Figure 2), so we can divide out the smoothing safely:

<M d tag="5" id="eq-l08-longk" t="\hat\Phi_{\rm long}(\vect k)=-\frac{4\pi G}{k^2}\,\frac{e^{-k^2r_s^2}}{W^2(\vect k)}\,\hat\rho(\vect k)." />

Figure 3 shows the effect: without the deconvolution the CIC mesh force is <M t="12" />&ndash;<M t="15\%" /> too small below <M t="2h" /> (TSC: <M t="20\%" />); with it, the error is about <M t="2\%" /> at worst and about <M t="0.1\%" /> beyond <M t="4h" />. The residual comes mostly from the four-point finite-difference gradient (with a spectral gradient the TSC error is within <M t="0.4\%" />); the rest is aliasing, which the deconvolution does not remove. (Figure 3 shows the case without deconvolution for CIC only; TSC without deconvolution is about <M t="20\%" /> low.) (The reference, the expected long-range force, is the Ewald force minus the short-range part of Eq. (4), since the mesh force is periodic.) The gradient and the interpolation are as in Lecture 6 (four-point gradient); the potential solver uses the spectral <M t="k^2" />.

<Callout kind="idea">
The mesh only has to be right where the short-range force has faded, at <M t="r\gtrsim2\,r_s" />. The Gaussian filter ensures that: it removes the grid-scale structure, so the mesh part is smooth and its error is small. That is why the mesh can be as coarse as <M t="r_s\approx1.25" /> cells.
</Callout>

## The short-range part

### P3M: a chaining mesh

The short-range force of a particle needs the neighbours closer than <M t="r_{\rm cut}" />. Sort the particles into cells of side at least <M t="r_{\rm cut}" /> (the **chaining mesh**, a different, coarser grid than the PM mesh); the neighbours of a particle then lie in its own cell and the <M t="26" /> adjacent ones (Figure 4). This is the pair search you wrote for friends-of-friends in Lecture 7, with <M t="r_{\rm cut}" /> in place of the linking length; the box is periodic, so the distance is the minimum-image distance, <M t="\vect d\leftarrow\vect d-L\,\mathrm{round}(\vect d/L)" />. Three practical points. (i) To find the pairs of two cells with no Python loop over particles, sort the particles by cell index, note where each cell starts (`np.searchsorted`) and how many particles it holds, and for each of the <M t="27" /> neighbour offsets build the flat index arrays of all (particle in cell <M t="A" />, particle in neighbour cell <M t="B" />) combinations at once (`np.repeat` of the cell numbers by the product of the two counts, and a running index within each block); then filter by distance. (ii) If <M t="L/r_{\rm cut}<3" /> the same neighbour cell is reached through several offsets, so a pair can be listed more than once: list each pair once (for example keep <M t="i<j" /> and remove duplicates). (iii) Accumulate the forces with `np.bincount`, adding <M t="\vect f_{ij}" /> to particle <M t="i" /> and subtracting it from <M t="j" />, so that Newton's third law and momentum conservation hold exactly. The minimum-image distance needs <M t="r_{\rm cut}\le L/2" />.

The short-range force on particle <M t="i" /> is

<M d tag="6" id="eq-l08-pp" t="\vect a_i=G\sum_{j:\,r_{ij}<r_{\rm cut}}m_j\,S(r_{ij})\,\frac{\vect d_{ij}}{(r_{ij}^2+\epsilon^2)^{3/2}},\qquad\vect d_{ij}=\vect x_j-\vect x_i\ \text{(minimum image)}," />

with the Plummer softening <M t="\epsilon" /> of Lecture 1 (softening acts only on the short-range part). The total force is the sum of the mesh force and Eq. (6). <M t="S" /> is evaluated with the true <M t="r_{ij}" /> (not the softened one), and the sum is truncated sharply at <M t="r_{\rm cut}" />, which leaves a <M t="1.8\%" /> jump in the force at the cutoff.

```python title="s3_cells_1d.py"
import numpy as np

L, rcut, N = 10.0, 0.8, 300
x = np.random.default_rng(1).random(N) * L
nc = int(L / rcut)                             # cells at least rcut wide
cell = (x / (L / nc)).astype(int) % nc
order = np.argsort(cell)
start = np.searchsorted(cell[order], np.arange(nc + 1))    # particles of cell c: order[start[c]:start[c+1]]
count = 0
for c in range(nc):
    mine = order[start[c]:start[c + 1]]
    near = np.concatenate([order[start[(c + s) % nc]:start[(c + s) % nc + 1]] for s in (-1, 0, 1)])
    d = x[near][None, :] - x[mine][:, None]
    d -= L * np.rint(d / L)                    # minimum image
    count += np.sum(np.abs(d) < rcut) - len(mine)           # minus self pairs
d = x[:, None] - x[None, :]
d -= L * np.rint(d / L)
print(count // 2, (np.sum(np.abs(d) < rcut) - N) // 2)      # same pair count twice
```

The listing shows cell lists in 1D finding the same pairs as a brute-force search (a toy).

The parameters trade accuracy against cost. A cluster of <M t="400" /> particles (eight Gaussian blobs of width <M t="0.03\,L" />; Figure 6) compared with the Ewald forces gives a median error of <M t="0.15" />&ndash;<M t="0.2\%" /> (maximum <M t="0.3" />&ndash;<M t="0.8\%" />) for <M t="r_s=1.25\,h" /> and TSC, <M t="0.02\%" /> for <M t="r_s=2\,h" />, and <M t="0.35" />&ndash;<M t="0.4\%" /> for <M t="r_s=1\,h" />. But the cost grows with <M t="r_{\rm cut}^3" />: a particle has <M t="(4\pi/3)\bar n\,r_{\rm cut}^3" /> neighbours at mean number density <M t="\bar n" />. Larger <M t="r_s" /> improves the accuracy by shifting work from the mesh error to the neighbour sum.

### A worked example and rules for the parameters

Take the box of Lecture 7: <M t="L=100\,h^{-1}" />Mpc, a <M t="64^3" /> mesh, <M t="32^3" /> particles. Then

| quantity | value |
|:---|---:|
| mesh cell <M t="h=L/n" /> | <M t="1.56\,h^{-1}" />Mpc |
| <M t="r_s=1.25\,h" /> | <M t="1.95\,h^{-1}" />Mpc |
| <M t="r_{\rm cut}=4.5\,r_s" /> | <M t="8.8\,h^{-1}" />Mpc |
| chaining-mesh cells (<M t="\ge r_{\rm cut}" />) | <M t="11^3" /> |
| mean particle number density <M t="\bar n" /> | <M t="0.033\,(h/\mathrm{Mpc})^3" /> |
| neighbours per particle, uniform: <M t="\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\bar nr_{\rm cut}^3" /> | <M t="93" /> |
| the same at <M t="100\times" /> the mean density | <M t="9\,300" /> |
| measured at <M t="a=1" /> (average) | <M t="179" /> |

so the pair sum at <M t="a=1" /> costs about <M t="179\,N/2\approx3\times10^6" /> pair evaluations against <M t="\Oh{N}+\Oh{n^3\log n}" /> for the mesh (the pure-NumPy P3M force takes about <M t="1.5" />&ndash;<M t="2" /> s against <M t="20" /> ms for PM).

The parameters are tied together by four rules. **(i)** The mesh must be accurate where the short-range force has faded (<M t="S\lesssim0.5" />, <M t="r\gtrsim2r_s" />), and the Gaussian must remove modes near <M t="k_N" />: <M t="r_s\approx1.25" /> cells does both. A larger <M t="r_s" /> makes the mesh part more accurate (Figure 6) but the pair sum more expensive. **(ii)** <M t="r_{\rm cut}" /> is chosen where <M t="S" /> is small, <M t="4.5\,r_s" />; it must be at most <M t="L/2" /> for the minimum-image convention (so the mesh cannot be smaller than about <M t="12^3" /> at the default <M t="r_s" />). **(iii)** The softening <M t="\epsilon" /> acts only on the short-range force and must be well below <M t="r_s" /> (we use <M t="0.05\times" /> the mean particle spacing, about <M t="0.15" /> Mpc/<M t="h" /> here, against <M t="r_s=1.95" />); the mesh force is unsoftened but smooth. **(iv)** Use about two mesh cells per mean particle spacing (a <M t="64^3" /> mesh for <M t="32^3" /> particles): coarser meshes push work to the pair sum, finer ones add noise.

### The weakness of P3M: clustering

The neighbour count is per particle, <M t="\propto\rho_{\rm local}\,r_{\rm cut}^3" />: in a dense halo, where <M t="\rho_{\rm local}" /> is thousands of times the mean, one particle can have thousands of neighbours within <M t="r_{\rm cut}" />, and the cost of the short-range sum grows towards <M t="N_{\rm halo}^2" />. This is what the tree is for.

## Testing a hybrid code

A hybrid code has many places to go wrong, and errors in one part are hidden by the other. Test from the bottom up. **(1)** The split function: <M t="S(0)=1" />, <M t="S(r)r^{-2}" /> equals minus the derivative of <M t="\mathrm{erfc}(r/2r_s)/r" />. **(2)** Each part alone: the mesh force between two particles should equal the Ewald force minus <M t="Gm\,S/r^2" />, and the short-range sum should equal a brute-force <M t="O(N^2)" /> sum over all pairs. **(3)** The total: the force between two particles against the Ewald force at many separations (Figure 5), and for a small clustered set against Ewald (Ewald is expensive: about 60 test particles suffice). **(4)** Invariances: shifting all positions by a box length, momentum conservation (to roundoff, about <M t="10^{-15}" /> or better relative to the sum of the absolute forces, for mesh and pair sum), <M t="G" /> scaling. **(5)** The tree: TreePM with <M t="\theta\to0" /> must reproduce P<sup>3</sup>M.

Typical symptoms: an error that peaks at <M t="r\approx1" />&ndash;<M t="3h" /> means a missing window deconvolution or a wrong filter width; a smooth <M t="\sim2\%" /> error at <M t="r\approx4.5\,r_s" /> means the cutoff or <M t="S" />; errors only for particles near the box edges point to the minimum image; pairs counted twice appear as a factor of <M t="2" /> at small <M t="L/r_{\rm cut}" />.

## TreePM: a tree for the short-range part

Replace the chaining-mesh sum by a Barnes&ndash;Hut tree walk that is *truncated* at <M t="r_{\rm cut}" />. Your Lecture 4 walk needs three changes, all visible in the schematic of Figure 7:

1. **Prune:** skip a cell entirely if the smallest distance from the particle to the cell is greater than <M t="r_{\rm cut}" /> (see the cell-gap listing below). Nothing inside can contribute.
2. **Cut and weight:** a cell accepted by the opening criterion is used as a point mass *with the short-range factor*: <M t="\vect a=Gm\,S(r)\,\vect d/(r^2+\epsilon^2)^{3/2}" /> evaluated at the distance <M t="r" /> to its centre of mass (skipped if <M t="r>r_{\rm cut}" />); the same for particles in a leaf.
3. **Periodicity:** use the minimum-image distance to a cell centre, to its centre of mass, and to particles. Cells larger than half the box cannot be pruned this way; open them (the root always contains the particle).

Everything else (tree build, opening criterion, stack) is your Lecture 4 code. Because only the short-range force is needed, the walk visits only cells near the particle, and its cost is smaller than for the full force.

```python title="s4_cell_gap.py"
import numpy as np

def gap_to_cell(p, centre, half, box):
    """Smallest distance (minimum image) from point p to the cube of half-width `half` around `centre`."""
    dc = centre - p
    dc -= box * np.rint(dc / box)
    gap = np.maximum(np.abs(dc) - half, 0.0)
    return np.linalg.norm(gap)

box, rcut = 10.0, 1.0
p = np.array([9.8, 5.0, 5.0])
print(round(gap_to_cell(p, np.array([0.6, 5.0, 5.0]), 0.5, box), 3))   # 0.3: the cell just across the boundary is near
print(gap_to_cell(p, np.array([5.0, 5.0, 5.0]), 0.5, box) > rcut)   # True: skip this cell
```

The listing computes the distance from a point to a cell with the minimum image (a fragment).

**Accuracy.** Against the Ewald force on the clustered test set (eight blobs, <M t="N=400" />), the TreePM force at <M t="\theta=0.5" /> has a median error <M t="0.4\%" />, <M t="90\%" /> of the particles below <M t="1.3\%" /> and a maximum of <M t="2" />&ndash;<M t="3\%" />; at <M t="\theta=0.7" />: <M t="1.2" />&ndash;<M t="1.4\%" />, <M t="3\%" /> and <M t="6" />&ndash;<M t="13\%" />; at <M t="\theta=1" />: <M t="3.6" />&ndash;<M t="3.7\%" /> (maximum <M t="16" />&ndash;<M t="54\%" />). Unlike the mesh and pair parts, the tree force does not conserve momentum exactly (the monopole interaction of a cell is not pairwise antisymmetric): at <M t="\theta=0.5" /> the net force is about <M t="10^{-4}" /> of the sum of the absolute forces.

**Cost.** Figure 8 counts the short-range interactions per particle. For *uniform* particles the tree does not pay: the pair sum needs fewer interactions at these <M t="N" />. In a cosmological run late in time, most particles are in clumps, so this is the regime that matters. (Our pure-Python tree is far too slow for the full box; in a compiled code the short-range tree is very fast, and, as we will see in Lecture 10, its cost per particle is low enough to update only the particles that need it in each step.)

## The hybrid force in a cosmological run

The hybrid force can be used in the comoving leapfrog of Lecture 7 unchanged: the acceleration is <M t="-\nabla\tilde\phi" /> with <M t="G=3\Omega_mH_0^2L^3/(8\pi N)" />, and the kick and drift factors are the same. Two things need care. **Softening:** with a resolved short-range force the particles interact like real point masses, and two-body encounters between the few particles per halo scatter and heat them. Take <M t="\epsilon" /> at a few per cent of the mean particle spacing (we use <M t="\epsilon=0.05\,L/N^{1/3}" />). **Time step:** the short-range force changes fast in dense regions: a particle on a bound orbit needs <M t="\Delta t\lesssim\eta\sqrt{\epsilon/|\vect a|}" /> (with <M t="\eta\approx0.02" />&ndash;<M t="0.05" />), which is far smaller than a step that suits the smooth mesh force. A single global step would be set by the densest clump; Lecture 10 introduces individual (block) time steps, where the short-range tree makes updating only the particles that need it cheap.

To see what the short-range force adds, we evolve the <M t="32^3" />-particle, <M t="100\,h^{-1}" />Mpc box of Lecture 7 to <M t="a=1" /> with PM alone and with P3M (mesh <M t="64^3" />, TSC, <M t="r_s=1.25h" />, <M t="\epsilon=0.05\,\bar d" />), and compare (Figure 9):

- *Forces:* on the final PM particle positions, the PM force magnitude relative to the P3M force is <M t="1.0" /> in regions near the mean density, <M t="0.93" /> at <M t="3" />&ndash;<M t="10" /> times the mean, <M t="0.49" /> at <M t="10" />&ndash;<M t="30" />, <M t="0.34" /> at <M t="30" />&ndash;<M t="100" /> and <M t="0.27" /> above <M t="100" />: **the mesh force in dense regions is a factor of three or four too small**, which is the resolution limit of Lecture 6.
- *Power spectrum:* the two runs agree to <M t="\lesssim8\%" /> for <M t="k<1.3\,h/" />Mpc: <M t="P(k)" /> on these scales is dominated by the large-scale structure, which the mesh gets right. (P<sup>3</sup>M has <M t="4" />&ndash;<M t="8\%" /> less power at <M t="k\approx0.3" />&ndash;<M t="1" />, and up to about <M t="10\%" /> more at <M t="k=1.3" />&ndash;<M t="2" />, near the mesh Nyquist wavenumber.) The two-body scattering of the sparse particle set is part of this; a sparser run (<M t="16^3" /> particles on a <M t="32^3" /> mesh) differs by <M t="20" />&ndash;<M t="40\%" /> at <M t="k\approx0.3" />&ndash;<M t="0.6" />, so the comparison is only meaningful when the particle number is adequate.
- *Halos:* FoF finds <M t="75" /> halos of <M t="\ge20" /> particles in the PM run and <M t="65" /> in the P3M run, with the largest at <M t="358" /> and <M t="352" /> particles.

The cost is the price of the accuracy: on our machine one P3M force at <M t="a=1" /> with this pure-NumPy code takes about <M t="1.5" />&ndash;<M t="2" /> s and the 60-step run about <M t="2" />&ndash;<M t="4" /> minutes, against a few seconds for the PM run (timings vary with the machine).

## Summary

- The Gaussian split <M t="1/r=\mathrm{erf}(r/2r_s)/r+\mathrm{erfc}(r/2r_s)/r" /> gives a smooth long-range part (filter <M t="e^{-k^2r_s^2}" /> on the mesh) and a short-range force <M t="Gm\,S(r)/r^2" /> that is negligible beyond <M t="r_{\rm cut}\approx4.5\,r_s" />.
- The mesh part needs the window deconvolved twice (assign and interpolate), <M t="\hat\rho\to\hat\rho\,e^{-k^2r_s^2}/W^2" />; then the total force is accurate to about <M t="1\%" /> at any separation.
- P<sup>3</sup>M sums the short-range force over neighbours from a chaining mesh (minimum image, Newton's third law); TreePM uses a tree pruned at <M t="r_{\rm cut}" />, with <M t="S(r)" /> applied to every interaction.
- <M t="r_s" /> and <M t="r_{\rm cut}" /> trade accuracy for cost (<M t="\propto r_{\rm cut}^3" /> neighbours); the tree wins where particles are clustered.
- In a cosmological box the PM force in dense regions is a factor three or four too small; the hybrid fixes it, at a cost that is much higher than PM alone.
