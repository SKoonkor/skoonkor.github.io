## The idea: let a grid do the work

The tree of Lectures 4&ndash;5 approximates the sum <M t="\sum_jm_j/r_{ij}^2" />. The **particle&ndash;mesh** method attacks the other form of the gravitational problem, the differential equation

<M d tag="1" id="eq-l06-poisson" t="\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho ." />

Spread the particles' mass on a regular grid to get the density <M t="\rho" />, solve Eq. (1) on the grid with a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), differentiate the potential to get the force on the grid, and interpolate that force back to the particles. None of these steps looks at pairs of particles: the cost is <M t="\Oh{N}" /> for the particle steps plus <M t="\Oh{N_g\log N_g}" /> for the FFT on the <M t="N_g=n^3" /> grid cells (<M t="n" /> cells per side), independent of how the particles are clustered.

<Callout kind="history" title="History">
Grid-based Poisson solvers for particle simulations were developed in plasma physics in the 1960s and systematised in R. Hockney & J. Eastwood, *Computer Simulation Using Particles* (1981). Particle&ndash;mesh gravity was applied to cosmological structure formation in the early 1980s (for example by Klypin & Shandarin 1983). It remains the long-range part of nearly every large simulation code (Lecture 8).
</Callout>

## Poisson's equation in Fourier space

Work in a periodic cube of side <M t="L" /> (the cosmological set-up; PM handles periodicity for free) with <M t="n^3" /> grid nodes at <M t="\vect x_g=(i,j,k)\,h" />, <M t="h=L/n" />. Particles are labelled by <M t="j" /> and grid nodes by <M t="g" />.

### Fourier conventions you need

A Fourier transform writes a field as a sum of plane waves <M t="e^{i\vect k\cdot\vect x}" />. On a periodic grid the allowed wave vectors are <M t="\vect k=\frac{2\pi}{L}(m_x,m_y,m_z)" /> with integer <M t="m" />. The discrete transform that the FFT computes is

<M d tag="2" id="eq-l06-dft" t="\hat\rho_{\vect m}=\sum_{i,j,k}\rho_{ijk}\,e^{-2\pi i(m_xi+m_yj+m_zk)/n}," />

which is what `np.fft.fftn` does, *without* a normalisation factor; `np.fft.ifftn` includes the factor <M t="1/n^3" />, so the inverse undoes the forward transform. (Relating <M t="\hat\rho_{\vect m}" /> to a continuous Fourier transform brings in a factor <M t="h^3" />: <M t="\hat\rho(\vect k)\approx h^3\hat\rho_{\vect m}" />; Lecture 7 needs this for power spectra.) Three facts matter:

- **Derivatives become products.** Since <M t="\partial_xe^{ikx}=ik\,e^{ikx}" />, differentiating multiplies each mode by <M t="ik_x" />, and <M t="\nabla^2" /> multiplies by <M t="-k^2" />. That is why Poisson's equation is easy in Fourier space.
- **The grid has a shortest wave.** At least two points per wavelength are needed: the **Nyquist wavenumber** is <M t="k_N=\pi/h" />. A wave with <M t="k>k_N" /> looks on the grid exactly like a lower-<M t="k" /> wave (**aliasing**).
- **Layout and symmetry.** `np.fft.fftfreq(n, d=h)` returns (for even <M t="n" />) the frequencies in the order <M t="0,1,\dots,n/2-1,-n/2,\dots,-1" /> (in units of <M t="1/nh" />); multiply by <M t="2\pi" /> to get <M t="k" />. For a *real* field <M t="\hat\rho(-\vect k)=\hat\rho(\vect k)^*" />, which `np.fft.rfftn` exploits by storing half of the modes (Lecture 7 uses the same symmetry when it generates real random fields).

### Solving Poisson's equation

Multiplication by <M t="-k^2" /> turns Eq. (1) into an algebraic equation,

<M d tag="3" id="eq-l06-phik" t="-k^2\,\hat\Phi(\vect k)=4\pi G\,\hat\rho(\vect k)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\hat\Phi(\vect k)=-\frac{4\pi G}{k^2}\,\hat\rho(\vect k)." />

The FFT computes <M t="\hat\rho" /> in <M t="\Oh{n^3\log n}" /> operations, and an inverse FFT gives <M t="\Phi" /> back (take the real part). The factor <M t="-4\pi G/k^2" /> is the Fourier transform of the **Green function**, the response of <M t="\Phi" /> to a point mass. The <M t="\vect k=0" /> mode needs care: <M t="\hat\rho(0)" /> is the mean density and Eq. (3) would divide by zero. In a periodic box a nonzero mean density has no consistent potential (the divergence that the neutralising background of the Ewald sum in Lecture 5 removes), so set <M t="\hat\Phi(0)=0" />: the potential is that of the *density contrast*. PM therefore solves the same periodic problem as the Ewald sum, with the same <M t="k=0" /> convention, but by a band-limited sum of the bare <M t="1/k^2" /> over the grid's modes with the kernel-smoothed density, instead of Ewald's real/Fourier split. The result is the periodic force of particles smoothed over roughly one grid cell.

```python title="s2_fft_poisson_1d.py"
import numpy as np

n, box, G = 64, 1.0, 1.0
x = np.arange(n) * box / n
rho = 1.0 + 0.5 * np.cos(2 * np.pi * 3 * x / box)      # a mean density plus one wave
k = 2 * np.pi * np.fft.fftfreq(n, d=box / n)           # wave numbers of the FFT modes
rho_k = np.fft.fft(rho)
k2 = k**2
k2[0] = 1.0                                            # avoid 0/0 for the k = 0 mode
phi_k = -4 * np.pi * G * rho_k / k2
phi_k[0] = 0.0                                         # the mean density is dropped
phi = np.real(np.fft.ifft(phi_k))
k3 = 2 * np.pi * 3 / box
print(np.abs(phi + 4 * np.pi * G * 0.5 * np.cos(k3 * x) / k3**2).max() < 1e-12)   # True
```

The listing is a one-dimensional Poisson solver with `np.fft`. The 3D version is Homework 6.2.

In 3D use `np.fft.fftn` and `ifftn` and build the <M t="k" /> arrays with `np.meshgrid(k, k, k, indexing='ij')` (or by broadcasting), so that the index order stays `[ix, iy, iz]`.

**Which <M t="k^2" />?** The grid only samples fields at the nodes, so there is a choice of what "<M t="k^2" />" means. In the code it is the argument `kernel` (the *Laplacian rule*; not to be confused with the assignment kernels of the next section). Two are common:

- the **spectral** value <M t="k^2=k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2" />;
- the **finite-difference** value of the three-point discrete Laplacian <M t="(\Phi_{i+1}+\Phi_{i-1}-2\Phi_i)/h^2" />, whose eigenvalue on a wave <M t="e^{ikx}" /> is <M t="-(4/h^2)\sin^2(kh/2)" />. Apply it per axis and add: <M t="k_{\rm eff}^2=\sum_{a=x,y,z}\big(\tfrac2h\sin\tfrac{k_ah}2\big)^2" />.

They agree for <M t="kh\ll1" /> and differ near the Nyquist wavenumber. The section on the force below shows that this choice matters much less than the choice of *gradient*.

<Callout kind="pitfall">
Getting the FFT conventions right is the main hurdle: the index order is `[ix, iy, iz]`; the inverse transform returns a complex array whose imaginary part is rounding noise (take `.real`); and the <M t="k=0" /> mode must be zeroed *after* you guard the division. Test your solver on a single cosine wave, whose exact answer you know.
</Callout>

## Mass assignment

To get <M t="\rho" /> on the grid, each particle shares its mass among nearby nodes with weights that sum to one. In one dimension, with a particle at <M t="x" /> and a node at <M t="x_g" />, the three standard **assignment kernels** <M t="W" />, in terms of <M t="s=|x_g-x|/h" />, are:

<M d tag="4" id="eq-l06-ngp" t="\text{NGP:}\quad W=1\ \text{ for }s<\tfrac12\qquad\text{(nearest node only)}" />

<M d tag="5" id="eq-l06-cic" t="\text{CIC:}\quad W=1-s\ \text{ for }s<1\qquad\text{(2 nodes; linear)}" />

<M d tag="6" id="eq-l06-tsc" t="\text{TSC:}\quad W=\tfrac34-s^2\ (s<\tfrac12),\ \ \tfrac12(\tfrac32-s)^2\ (\tfrac12\le s<\tfrac32)\qquad\text{(3 nodes; quadratic)}" />

In three dimensions the weight is the product <M t="W(x)W(y)W(z)" />, and the density at node <M t="g" /> is

<M d tag="7" id="eq-l06-assign" t="\rho_g=\frac{1}{h^3}\sum_jm_j\,W(x_g-x_j)\,W(y_g-y_j)\,W(z_g-z_j)." />

Nodes near the box edge wrap around periodically, and so do particles that have drifted out of the box.

```python title="s1_cic_1d.py"
import numpy as np

n, box = 8, 1.0
h = box / n                                   # grid nodes sit at 0, h, 2h, ...
x = 0.30                                      # one particle of mass 1 in a 1D box
u = x / h                                     # position in grid units: 2.4
i0 = int(np.floor(u))                         # node to the left: 2
d = u - i0                                    # fractional distance from it: 0.4
rho = np.zeros(n)
rho[i0 % n] += (1 - d) / h                    # left node gets weight 1 - d
rho[(i0 + 1) % n] += d / h                    # right node gets weight d
print(rho * h)                                # [0. 0. 0.6 0.4 0. 0. 0. 0.]
print(rho.sum() * h)                          # 1.0: mass is conserved
```

The listing computes the cloud-in-cell weights of one particle in one dimension.

**A recipe for the 3D version.** Per axis, compute the base node and weights: NGP uses the nearest node (`np.rint`); CIC the node to the left (`np.floor`) and its right neighbour; TSC the nearest node (`np.rint`, *not* floor) and its two neighbours. Then loop over the <M t="1" />, <M t="2^3" /> or <M t="3^3" /> combinations of per-axis offsets (*not* over particles), and for each combination add <M t="m_j\,w_xw_yw_z" /> into node <M t="(i_x,i_y,i_z)\bmod n" />. Scatter-adding many contributions into the same node needs `np.add.at` or, much faster, `np.bincount` on a flattened node index with `weights=...`. Interpolation is the mirror image: gather the grid values at the same indices and weight them with the same <M t="w" />.

Two properties matter. **Mass is conserved** (<M t="\sum_g\rho_gh^3=\sum_jm_j" /> because the weights sum to one). CIC and TSC also preserve the **centre of mass**: the mass-weighted mean node position equals the mean particle position, because they interpolate linearly; NGP does not. The grid cannot resolve structure below <M t="2h" />, and finer grids have fewer particles per cell and noisier <M t="\rho" /> (Figure 4).

## From potential to force on the particles

**The gradient on the grid.** With <M t="\vect a=-\nabla\Phi" /> we can differentiate spectrally (multiply <M t="\hat\Phi" /> by <M t="-i\vect k" />, dropping the Nyquist mode, whose derivative is not defined) or with a finite-difference stencil. The common four-point rule is

<M d tag="8" id="eq-l06-fd4" t="\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big|_i\approx\frac{8\,(f_{i+1}-f_{i-1})-(f_{i+2}-f_{i-2})}{12\,h}," />

accurate to <M t="\Oh{h^4}" />, using periodic neighbours (`np.roll`).

```python title="s3_fd_gradient.py"
import numpy as np

n, box = 32, 1.0
h = box / n
x = np.arange(n) * h
k = 2 * np.pi * 3 / box
f = np.cos(k * x)
# 4-point central difference: (8 f[i+1] - 8 f[i-1] - f[i+2] + f[i-2]) / (12 h)
df = (8 * (np.roll(f, -1) - np.roll(f, 1)) - (np.roll(f, -2) - np.roll(f, 2))) / (12 * h)
print(np.abs(df + k * np.sin(k * x)).max() / k)        # 0.0039: 0.4% of the amplitude
```

The listing applies the four-point gradient to a cosine wave.

**Interpolation back to the particles.** The force on particle <M t="j" /> is the grid force averaged with the *same* weights that were used for assignment:

<M d tag="9" id="eq-l06-interp" t="\vect a_j=\sum_g W(\vect x_g-\vect x_j)\,\vect a_g ." />

Interpolation is then the *transpose* ("adjoint") of assignment: for any grid field <M t="f" />, <M t="h^3\sum_g\rho_gf_g=\sum_jm_j\,f(\vect x_j)" />, with <M t="f(\vect x_j)" /> the interpolated value (see the adjoint listing below). This is not a matter of taste; it gives exact momentum conservation. Write <M t="\Phi=S\rho" /> and <M t="\vect a=-D\Phi" />, where <M t="S" /> (the Green function) is a symmetric matrix and <M t="D" /> (the gradient stencil, per axis) an antisymmetric one; both are translation-invariant, hence commute. The total force is

<M d tag="10" id="eq-l06-momentum" t="\sum_jm_j\vect a_j=h^3\sum_g\rho_g\vect a_g=-h^3\,\rho^{\rm T}DS\rho=0 ," />

where the first step uses the adjoint property, and the last holds because <M t="(DS)^{\rm T}=S^{\rm T}D^{\rm T}=-SD=-DS" /> is antisymmetric and <M t="\rho^{\rm T}M\rho=0" /> for antisymmetric <M t="M" />. **Total momentum is conserved** to rounding error. The same argument applied to a single particle shows that it exerts **no force on itself**.

```python title="s4_adjoint_toy.py"
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, box = 8, 1.0
h = box / n
x = rng.random(5) * box                                # five particles
m = rng.random(5) + 0.5
f = rng.normal(size=n)                                 # any field on the grid

def weights(x):                                        # 1D CIC: (left node, weight left, weight right)
    u = x / h
    i0 = np.floor(u).astype(int)
    return i0, 1 - (u - i0), u - i0

i0, wl, wr = weights(x)
rho = np.zeros(n)
np.add.at(rho, i0 % n, m * wl / h)                     # assign ...
np.add.at(rho, (i0 + 1) % n, m * wr / h)
f_at_particles = f[i0 % n] * wl + f[(i0 + 1) % n] * wr # ... and interpolate with the SAME weights
print(np.isclose((rho * f).sum() * h, (m * f_at_particles).sum()))   # True
```

Assignment and interpolation are adjoint (the listing is a 1D CIC example).

<Callout kind="idea">
Use the same weights for assignment and interpolation. Mixing them (say CIC assignment with NGP interpolation) breaks the adjoint property, gives a spurious self-force and violates momentum conservation. Your checker tests both.
</Callout>

```python title="s5_wrap.py"
import numpy as np

box, n = 1.0, 8
pos = np.array([1.23, -0.10, 0.999])                   # particles may drift outside the box
print(pos % box)                                       # [0.23 0.9  0.999]: wrapped back in
idx = np.array([-1, 0, 7, 8])
print(idx % n)                                         # [7 0 7 0]: grid indices wrap too
```

The listing shows how to wrap positions and grid indices.

## How accurate is the PM force?

The PM force is the force between particles smeared over roughly one grid cell, so it is smoothed at small separations <M t="r\lesssim3h" />. At <M t="r\gtrsim3h" /> it should be close to Newton's <M t="Gm/r^2" /> (plus periodic corrections that are small for <M t="r\ll L" />). We measure it with a pair: a heavy source and a light probe (mass <M t="10^{-6}" />) at separation <M t="r" />, at random positions and directions, compared with the exact periodic force (Ewald, Lecture 5).

A particle exactly on a node deposits all its mass on one node: the density is a spike. The spectral derivative of a grid spike is the slowly decaying, alternating tail of Figure 6. The Poisson step turns the spike into a smooth potential, but differentiating that potential spectrally leaves the alternating tail in the force (a finite-difference Laplacian does not cure it). A particle midway between nodes is two half-weight spikes whose tails largely cancel. The four-point rule has no such tail, and with it either Laplacian rule gives a force close to <M t="-1" /> in the same test. So the *gradient* rule, not the Laplacian rule, is what matters; and the effect depends on direction and offset, so do not expect it to look the same for every pair.

## Cost

Assignment and interpolation cost <M t="\Oh{N}" /> each; the FFT costs <M t="\Oh{n^3\log n}" />. For <M t="N=10^5" /> particles on a <M t="32^3" /> grid one force evaluation takes about <M t="26" /> ms in NumPy; with few particles the FFT dominates (a <M t="64^3" /> grid takes <M t="\sim13" /> ms even for <M t="10^3" /> particles).

The price is **resolution**: the force below <M t="\sim3h" /> is smoothed; each grid array has <M t="n^3" /> floats (<M t="64^3" />: <M t="2" /> MB; <M t="512^3" />: <M t="1" /> GB; <M t="4096^3" />: <M t="550" /> GB per array, and a run needs several); and a fixed grid cannot follow a density that spans many orders of magnitude, as in a galaxy halo. Adaptive meshes and multigrid solvers are one answer (Lecture 10 mentions them). Lecture 8 instead combines PM for large scales with a tree or direct sum for short scales, splitting the force with a Gaussian filter: the mesh keeps <M t="\hat\Phi_{\rm long}=-\frac{4\pi G}{k^2}e^{-k^2r_s^2}\hat\rho" />.

## PM in a simulation

The PM force has the interface of your Lecture 3 force, `force(pos, mass, G, eps)`, so it plugs into `evolve_kdk`. Two remarks. First, `eps` is ignored: the grid itself softens the force. Second, particles may leave the box; the force wraps them back, so periodic boundaries cost nothing. In a test of <M t="300" /> Plummer particles for <M t="100" /> steps the total momentum changes by <M t="\sim10^{-17}" />, as with direct summation and unlike the tree, and the Lagrangian radii agree with the direct sum softened at <M t="\epsilon=h/2" /> to <M t="1" />&ndash;<M t="2\%" /> for all the radii from <M t="10" /> to <M t="90\%" /> of the mass. Do not expect PM to match a Newtonian force below <M t="\sim3h" />: the smoothing is a property of the method, not a bug.

## Summary

- PM assigns mass to a grid (NGP, CIC, TSC), solves <M t="\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho" /> with an FFT (dropping the mean), differentiates, and interpolates the force back with the same weights.
- The same weights in both directions give exact momentum conservation and no self-force; periodicity is automatic.
- The force is smoothed below <M t="\sim3h" />; the grid acts like a softening of order <M t="h/2" /> to <M t="h" />.
- Use CIC or TSC with the four-point gradient. A spectral gradient turns a grid spike into an alternating <M t="1/j" /> tail and gives wrong-sign, oversized forces for particles near nodes: a fine median but a terrible worst case.
- Cost is <M t="\Oh{N}+\Oh{n^3\log n}" /> per force: a <M t="10^5" />-particle force in tens of milliseconds, at the price of fixed resolution (Lecture 8 adds short-range forces).
