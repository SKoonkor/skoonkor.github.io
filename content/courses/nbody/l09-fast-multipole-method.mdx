## Beyond the tree: interactions between cells

A Barnes&ndash;Hut tree (Lectures 4&ndash;5) lets each particle interact with a few hundred cells instead of <M t="N" /> particles: the cost is <M t="O(N\log N)" />. Yet a particle still walks the tree on its own, and two neighbouring particles repeat almost the same walk. The fast multipole method removes this redundancy. It makes two changes:

1. **Local expansions.** Instead of evaluating a cell's multipole expansion at each target particle, evaluate it *once* at the centre of a target *cell*, as a Taylor series in the distance from that centre (a *local expansion*); the particles of the target cell then only evaluate this Taylor series.
2. **Hierarchical translation.** Expansions are moved from a cell to its parent (multipole-to-multipole) and from a parent to its children (local-to-local) exactly, using algebraic formulas; then a cell needs the far field only from cells of a similar size.

The result is **cell&ndash;cell** interactions, with a fixed number of them per cell: the total work is proportional to the number of cells, hence to <M t="N" />.

**Who supplies which part of the far field?** Without a hierarchy, every cell would need the field of every well-separated cell (at leaf level, <M t="\sim N^2/n^2" /> pairs). The hierarchy splits the far field of a leaf by distance: the cells *just beyond its neighbours* (the interaction list of Figure 4, all of similar size to the leaf) come in by M2L at the leaf level; the cells beyond those were already well separated from the leaf's *parent*, so they came in by M2L at the parent's level, and reach the leaf through the L2L shift from the parent; and so on up the ancestors. Each interaction is done once, at the coarsest level where the two cells are well separated, and every cell only ever does a constant number (<M t="\le27" />) of M2L operations.

<Callout kind="history" title="History">
Appel (1985) and Barnes & Hut (1986) put particles into hierarchical trees; Greengard & Rokhlin (1987) added local expansions and the translation operators, giving a method with <M t="O(N)" /> cost and controllable accuracy. Dongarra & Sullivan (*Computing in Science & Engineering*, 2000) listed it among the ten most influential algorithms of the 20th century. Carrier, Greengard & Rokhlin (1988) made it adaptive to clustered particle sets and Cheng, Greengard & Rokhlin (1999) gave an adaptive 3D version. In astrophysics, Dehnen (2000, 2002) introduced a momentum-conserving tree code and a fast dual-tree FMM (falcON) for softened gravity, and Dehnen (2014) a cache-friendly FMM for stellar dynamics; GADGET-4 (Springel et al. 2021) includes an FMM for its short-range force.
</Callout>

## Two-dimensional gravity with complex numbers

We work in 2D. A particle at <M t="z_j=x_j+iy_j" /> with mass <M t="q_j" /> attracts a point <M t="z" /> with the two-dimensional force law <M t="\propto1/r" /> (the field of an infinite line mass), i.e. with acceleration <M t="G\,q_j\,\hat{\vect r}/r" />. The trick of the method is that the whole force can be written as the conjugate of one *analytic* function,

<M d tag="1" id="eq-l09-f" t="f(z)=\sum_j\frac{q_j}{z-z_j},\qquad a(z)=-G\,\overline{f(z)}," />

(check: <M t="-G\,\overline{q/(z-z_j)}=-Gq\,(z-z_j)/|z-z_j|^2" /> points from <M t="z" /> towards <M t="z_j" /> and has magnitude <M t="Gq/r" />). Because <M t="f" /> is analytic away from the sources, it has convergent power-series expansions, and all operators become polynomial algebra. The lessons carry over to 3D, where one uses real spherical harmonics or Cartesian Taylor tensors instead of powers of <M t="z" /> (see the section on clustered particles and 3D gravity below).

## The expansions

### Multipole expansion (P2M)

Let the sources lie in a disc of radius <M t="r" /> around a centre <M t="z_c" /> and let <M t="|z-z_c|=R>r" />. Write <M t="z-z_j=(z-z_c)-(z_j-z_c)" /> and expand the geometric series:

<M d tag="2" id="eq-l09-geom" t="\frac{1}{z-z_j}=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{(z_j-z_c)^k}{(z-z_c)^{k+1}}+\frac{(z_j-z_c)^p}{(z-z_c)^p\,(z-z_j)} ." />

Summing over the sources gives the **multipole expansion** with <M t="p" /> terms and the exact remainder:

<M d tag="3" id="eq-l09-multipole" t="f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{c_k}{(z-z_c)^{k+1}}+\text{remainder},\qquad c_k=\sum_jq_j\,(z_j-z_c)^k,\qquad|\text{remainder}|\le\frac{\sum_j|q_j|}{R-r}\Big(\frac rR\Big)^p ." />

The coefficients <M t="c_k" /> are the *moments* of the cell, computed once from its particles; the bound follows from <M t="|z-z_j|\ge R-r" /> (Figure 3, left, shows it holds for real data). The connection to Lecture 5 is direct: <M t="c_0=\sum q_j" /> is the mass (the monopole); <M t="c_1=\sum q_j(z_j-z_c)" /> vanishes if <M t="z_c" /> is the centre of mass (no dipole about the centre of mass); <M t="c_2=\sum q_j(z_j-z_c)^2" /> is the (traceless) quadrupole about the centre, built from <M t="x^2-y^2" /> and <M t="xy" />. Truncating after <M t="p" /> terms is what limits accuracy: the bound is the geometric-series remainder of the listing below.

```python title="s2_geometric.py"
import numpy as np

# 1/(1 - x) = sum_k x^k: truncating after p terms leaves x^p / (1 - x). This is the origin of every error bound.
x = 0.5 + 0.2j
exact = 1 / (1 - x)
for p in (2, 4, 8, 16):
    partial = sum(x**k for k in range(p))
    print(p, abs(exact - partial), abs(x) ** p / abs(1 - x))       # the bound is attained up to a phase
```

The remainder of a geometric series, <M t="x^p/(1-x)" />, is the origin of every error bound.

### Moving a multipole expansion (M2M)

To use the moments of the four children in the parent, shift the centre. With <M t="z_j-z_{\rm new}=(z_j-z_{\rm old})+(z_{\rm old}-z_{\rm new})=(z_j-z_{\rm old})+d" />, the binomial theorem gives

<M d tag="4" id="eq-l09-m2m" t="c_k^{\rm new}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom km\,c_m^{\rm old}\,d^{\,k-m},\qquad d=z_{\rm old}-z_{\rm new}." />

This is *exact* (no truncation error beyond the <M t="p" /> terms already dropped): the shifted coefficients equal those you would obtain by a P2M from scratch about the new centre. For <M t="k=2" /> it reads <M t="c_2'=c_2+2c_1d+c_0d^2" />; about the centre of mass (<M t="c_1=0" />) it reduces to <M t="c_2'=c_2+c_0d^2" />, the analogue of the parallel-axis theorem <M t="I=I_{\rm cm}+Md^2" /> of Lecture 5.

### Local expansion and M2L

Let a target cell have centre <M t="z_t" />, and let a multipole expansion about <M t="z_c" /> be given, with <M t="d=z_t-z_c" />. The series below converges for <M t="|w|<|d|" /> and the multipole series for all points farther than <M t="r" /> from <M t="z_c" />; so the M2L expansion is valid for target points <M t="z" /> with <M t="|z-z_t|<|d|-r" />, that is when the source and target discs do not overlap. Put <M t="w=z-z_t" />, so <M t="z-z_c=w+d" />, and expand each term in powers of <M t="w" /> (the binomial series of <M t="(1+w/d)^{-(k+1)}" />, obtained by differentiating the geometric series <M t="k" /> times; for <M t="k=0" /> it is <M t="1/(w+d)=\sum_l(-w)^l/d^{l+1}" />):

<M d tag="5" id="eq-l09-binom" t="\frac{1}{(w+d)^{k+1}}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(-1)^l\binom{k+l}{l}\frac{w^l}{d^{\,k+l+1}} ." />

Substituting into Eq. (3) gives the **local expansion** <M t="f(z)=\sum_lL_l\,w^l" /> with

<M d tag="6" id="eq-l09-m2l" t="L_l=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}c_k\,(-1)^l\binom{k+l}{l}\frac{1}{d^{\,k+l+1}},\qquad l=0,\dots,p-1." />

This is a matrix&ndash;vector product <M t="\vect L=T(d)\,\vect c" /> with a <M t="p\times p" /> matrix that depends only on <M t="d" />. The **M2L** operation is the heart of the FMM: it converts *all* the sources in one cell into a field expansion valid everywhere in another cell.

### Local shift (L2L) and evaluation (L2P)

A local expansion is a polynomial in <M t="w=z-z_{\rm old}" />; with the new centre at <M t="z_{\rm new}=z_{\rm old}+s" /> we have <M t="w=w'+s" />, where <M t="w'=z-z_{\rm new}" />, and <M t="(w'+s)^l=\sum_{m\le l}\binom lm w'^m s^{l-m}" />. Collecting the coefficient of <M t="w'^m" /> (so the sum over <M t="l" /> starts at <M t="m" />) gives an expansion that is exact for the truncated polynomial:

<M d tag="7" id="eq-l09-l2l" t="L^{\rm new}_m=\sum_{l=m}^{p-1}\binom lm\,L_l\,s^{\,l-m},\qquad s=z_{\rm new}-z_{\rm old}." />

At a leaf, the field of the far cells at a particle is <M t="f(z)=\sum_lL_l\,(z-z_t)^l" /> (**L2P**). Evaluating a multipole expansion directly, <M t="\sum_kc_k/(z-z_c)^{k+1}" />, is **M2P**, the operation used by a tree code.

```python title="s1_binomial.py"
from math import comb

# Shifting a centre needs (a + b)^k = sum_j C(k, j) a^j b^(k-j): the binomial theorem for complex numbers.
a, b, k = 0.3 + 0.2j, -0.5 + 0.1j, 5
lhs = (a + b) ** k
rhs = sum(comb(k, j) * a**j * b ** (k - j) for j in range(k + 1))
print(abs(lhs - rhs))                          # ~1e-17
```

The binomial theorem underlies both shifts (M2M and L2L).

### A worked example

Take two sources on the real axis: mass <M t="1" /> at <M t="z=1" /> and mass <M t="2" /> at <M t="z=-1" />, expanded about <M t="z_c=0" />. Then <M t="c_k=1\cdot1^k+2\cdot(-1)^k" />, i.e. <M t="c=(3,-1,3,-1,\dots)" />. Check every operator by hand (the exact values are <M t="f(5)=\tfrac14+\tfrac26=0.58333" />, <M t="f(10)=0.29293" />, <M t="f'(10)=-0.028875" />):

| operation | result (<M t="p=4" />) |
|:---|:---|
| P2M about <M t="0" /> | <M t="c=(3,\,-1,\,3,\,-1)" /> |
| M2P at <M t="z=5" />, terms <M t="p=1,2,3,4" /> | <M t="0.6,\ 0.56,\ 0.584,\ 0.5824" /> (exact <M t="0.58333" />) |
| M2M to the centre <M t="2" /> (<M t="d=-2" />) | <M t="c'=(3,\,-7,\,19,\,-55)" /> = P2M about <M t="2" /> |
| M2L to the centre <M t="10" /> (<M t="d=10" />) | <M t="L=(0.2929,\,-0.02886,\,0.00287,\,-0.000288)" /> (exact <M t="f(10)=0.29293" />, <M t="f'(10)=-0.028875" />) |

### How many terms?

For two boxes of side <M t="s" /> whose centres are at least <M t="2s" /> apart (one empty box in between), the source radius (half the diagonal) is <M t="r=s/\sqrt2" />, and the target points lie within the same distance of the target centre. The M2L series converges at least as fast as <M t="\big[r/(D-r)\big]^p=[0.71/(2-0.71)]^p\approx0.55^p" /> (Figure 3, right; this is a *bound*, and the observed decay is <M t="\approx0.4" /> per term, because the worst geometry is rare). So <M t="p\approx\ln\varepsilon/\ln0.55=1.7\ln(1/\varepsilon)" /> terms are always enough for relative accuracy <M t="\varepsilon" />: <M t="p=8" /> for <M t="10^{-2}" /> and <M t="p=16" /> for <M t="10^{-4}" />; in practice <M t="p\approx5" />&ndash;<M t="6" /> and <M t="11" />&ndash;<M t="12" /> suffice. The other operators add no error: M2M and L2L are exact for the truncated series, so the only approximation is the truncation of the multipole series in M2L.

## The tree and the interaction lists

Use a **uniform quadtree** of <M t="L" /> levels: level <M t="l" /> has <M t="2^l\times2^l" /> boxes, the box <M t="(i_x,i_y)" /> has centre <M t="((i_x+\tfrac12)/2^l,(i_y+\tfrac12)/2^l)" /> in the unit square, and each box has four children. Two boxes at the same level are **neighbours** if they touch (up to <M t="8" /> neighbours); non-neighbours are **well separated**. The **interaction list** of a box <M t="B" /> at level <M t="l" /> contains the children of the neighbours of <M t="B" />'s *parent* that are not neighbours of <M t="B" /> (Figure 4). In 2D, it has at most <M t="6\times6-3\times3=27" /> boxes (in 3D, <M t="189" />).

**Why this construction.** The children of the parent's neighbours are at most <M t="3" /> box widths away: close enough that their own parents were *not* well separated from <M t="B" />'s parent (so the interaction was not done at the coarser level), and, once the boxes of the list are separated from <M t="B" /> by at least one box, they satisfy the convergence condition of the previous section. Every pair of leaf boxes is therefore covered *exactly once*: either they are near neighbours (direct sum), or they are in the interaction list of one pair of ancestors. The 1D analogue is in the listing below (the counts <M t="3" />, <M t="27" />, <M t="189" /> are the sizes of the 1D, 2D, 3D lists), and your checker tests the partition property.

```python title="s3_lists_1d.py"
# Interaction lists in 1D: boxes 0..2^l-1; the parent of box i is i // 2.
# List(i) = children of the parent's neighbours that are not neighbours of i (and not i itself).
def interaction_list_1d(i, level):
    n = 2**level
    parent = i // 2
    kids = [c for pn in (parent - 1, parent, parent + 1) for c in (2 * pn, 2 * pn + 1) if 0 <= c < n]
    return [c for c in kids if abs(c - i) > 1]

print(interaction_list_1d(5, 3), interaction_list_1d(4, 3))   # [2, 3, 7] [2, 6, 7]: 3 boxes in 1D (27 in 2D, 189 in 3D)

# Exercise: show that every leaf is either a neighbour of leaf i or lies in the list of exactly one ancestor of i.
```

Interaction lists in 1D: at most 3 boxes. The near field plus the lists of all ancestors cover every leaf exactly once; write this test yourself.

## The algorithm

Given <M t="N" /> particles in the unit square, <M t="p" /> terms and <M t="L\ge2" /> levels:

1. **Sort** the particles into leaf boxes (the leaf-sorting listing below).
2. **Upward pass.** P2M at every non-empty leaf; for <M t="l=L-1,\dots,2" />, M2M from the four children into each box.
3. **Downward pass.** For <M t="l=2,\dots,L" /> and every box: <M t="\vect L\leftarrow" /> L2L of the parent's local expansion (for <M t="l>2" />) <M t="+\sum_{B'\in\text{list}(B)}" /> M2L<M t="(B'\to B)" />.
4. **Leaves.** For every particle in a leaf: L2P of the leaf's local expansion, plus the direct sum <M t="\sum_jq_j/(z_i-z_j)" /> over the particles of the leaf and of its (up to <M t="8" />) neighbours, excluding the particle itself.

The order matters: all M2M steps must be finished before the first M2L (a box's multipole expansion needs its children's), and the L2L shift from a parent must be added before the leaf is evaluated. The levels <M t="0" /> and <M t="1" /> have no well-separated pairs, so the passes start at level <M t="2" />. The M2L sources must lie inside the domain (boxes at the edge have shorter lists), empty boxes have zero coefficients and are harmless, and the direct sum must exclude a particle's own term but include its neighbours (the lists do not contain neighbours). The M2L matrices <M t="T(d)" /> of Eq. (6) depend only on the offset <M t="d" /> between the box centres, which takes a few dozen values per level; you can precompute them.

```text title="the passes in pseudocode"
sort particles into leaf boxes
for every leaf (ix, iy):                      # upward pass
    M[L][ix, iy] = P2M(particles of the leaf, centre of the leaf)
for l = L-1 down to 2:
    for every box (ix, iy) at level l:
        M[l][ix, iy] = sum over the 4 children of M2M(child, d = child centre - box centre)
for l = 2 up to L:                            # downward pass
    for every box (ix, iy) at level l:
        Loc[l][ix, iy] = 0
        if l > 2: Loc[l][ix, iy] += L2L(Loc[l-1][parent], s = box centre - parent centre)
        for (jx, jy) in interaction_list(ix, iy, l):
            Loc[l][ix, iy] += M2L(M[l][jx, jy], d = centre(ix, iy) - centre(jx, jy))
for every leaf (ix, iy) and every particle i in it:
    f[i] = L2P(Loc[L][ix, iy], z[i] - centre)
         + sum over particles j in the leaf and its neighbours, j != i, of q[j] / (z[i] - z[j])
```

The passes of the uniform-tree FMM in pseudocode (nothing here is Python; the operators are those of the section on the expansions).

```python title="s4_leaf_sort.py"
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
z = rng.random(1000) + 1j * rng.random(1000)   # particles in the unit square
levels = 3
n = 2**levels
ix = np.clip((z.real * n).astype(int), 0, n - 1)
iy = np.clip((z.imag * n).astype(int), 0, n - 1)
flat = ix * n + iy
order = np.argsort(flat, kind="stable")        # particles sorted by leaf box
start = np.searchsorted(flat[order], np.arange(n * n + 1))
def members(a, b):
    return order[start[a * n + b]:start[a * n + b + 1]]     # particles of box (a, b)

print(len(members(3, 4)), np.bincount(flat, minlength=n * n).reshape(n, n)[3, 4])   # same count twice
```

Sorting the particles into leaf boxes (2D).

**From the field to a simulation.** The FMM returns the complex field <M t="f(z_i)" />; the acceleration of particle <M t="i" /> is <M t="a_i=-G\,\overline{f(z_i)}" />, i.e. <M t="(a_x,a_y)=-G\,(\mathrm{Re}f,-\mathrm{Im}f)" />, which plugs into the leapfrog of Lecture 2 like any other force routine. ## Cost and accuracy

**Error against <M t="p" />.** For <M t="N=4000" /> uniformly distributed particles and <M t="L=4" /> (<M t="256" /> leaves), the error of <M t="f" /> relative to <M t="\sum_j|q_j/(z_i-z_j)|" /> (a scale that does not vanish where <M t="f" /> happens to cancel) is: <M t="p=2" />: median <M t="2\times10^{-3}" />, maximum <M t="10^{-2}" />; <M t="p=4" />: <M t="1\times10^{-4}" />, <M t="2\times10^{-3}" />; <M t="p=8" />: <M t="1.2\times10^{-6}" />, <M t="1.6\times10^{-5}" />; <M t="p=12" />: <M t="2\times10^{-8}" />, <M t="4\times10^{-7}" />; <M t="p=20" />: <M t="8\times10^{-12}" />, <M t="4\times10^{-10}" /> (Figure 5). The error falls geometrically with <M t="p" /> (about <M t="0.4" /> per term, inside the <M t="0.55" /> bound), so a few more terms buy many digits, unlike the tree, where accuracy comes from a smaller <M t="\theta" /> at a power-law growth in cost.

**Cost.** With <M t="4^L" /> leaves and <M t="n=N/4^L" /> particles per leaf, the near field costs about <M t="9\,n\,N" /> pair interactions (a leaf and its <M t="8" /> neighbours), and the far field costs <M t="27\cdot4^L\,p^2" /> operations for the M2L (each a <M t="p\times p" /> product) plus <M t="O(Np)" /> for P2M and L2P. (The M2L count <M t="27\cdot4^L" /> is an upper bound: boxes at the domain edge have fewer list entries, at most <M t="18" /> along an edge (<M t="15" /> for the other parity of the box index) and <M t="12" /> at a corner, and the coarser levels add another third.) Increasing <M t="L" /> moves work from the near to the far field (Figure 6); the total is smallest when the two are comparable, which for <M t="p\approx8" /> is a broad minimum at roughly <M t="10" />&ndash;<M t="50" /> particles per leaf (see the operation-count listing below); the run time of a Python implementation is flat over a range of <M t="L" /> around it. Keep <M t="n" /> constant as <M t="N" /> grows, i.e. <M t="L\approx\log_4(N/n)" />: the total is then **proportional to <M t="N" />**.

```python title="s5_opcount.py"
import numpy as np

N, p = 100_000, 8
for L in range(3, 9):
    boxes = 4**L
    per_box = N / boxes
    near = 9 * per_box * N                     # each particle meets ~9 boxes of ~per_box particles
    far = 27 * boxes * p**2 + 2 * N * p        # M2L (27 per box, p^2 each) + P2M and L2P
    print(L, f"{near:.2e} {far:.2e} {near + far:.2e}")     # the total is smallest at L = 6 (24 particles per leaf)
```

## Clustered particles and 3D gravity

**Clustering.** A *uniform* tree wastes work when particles are clustered: the leaves in a dense clump hold thousands of particles, and the near-field sum grows like <M t="n^2" /> for them, while most other leaves are empty (Figure 9). Adaptive FMMs (Carrier et al. 1988) refine only where the particles are, and use additional interaction lists for boxes at different levels. The tree of Lecture 4 (splitting cells until they hold at most a given number of particles) is the natural starting point.

**From 2D to 3D.** The method is the same in 3D: octree, interaction lists of up to <M t="189" /> boxes, expansions of the potential <M t="1/|\vect r-\vect r_j|" /> in spherical harmonics (Greengard & Rokhlin, 3D) or in Cartesian Taylor series. With expansions of order <M t="p" /> (terms up to <M t="(r/R)^p" />; in our 2D code the number of terms is <M t="p" />), the M2L cost grows as <M t="p^4" /> for the naive spherical-harmonic matrix (<M t="p^3" /> with rotations, <M t="p^2" /> with plane-wave expansions). Astrophysical codes use low orders, <M t="p\approx2" />&ndash;<M t="4" /> (quadrupole to hexadecapole) because <M t="10^{-3}" /> force accuracy suffices; and Plummer-softened forces are treated exactly in the near field, with the expansions taken for the unsoftened far field.

**The dual tree walk (Dehnen).** Instead of fixed interaction lists, one can traverse the tree recursively with a pair of cells <M t="(A,B)" />: *if* <M t="A" /> and <M t="B" /> are well separated (opening criterion on their sizes and distance), do the M2L (or a mutual direct interaction if both are small); *else* split the larger cell and recurse on the children. It needs no explicit lists, adapts to any tree, and, when interactions are computed symmetrically (<M t="A\to B" /> and <M t="B\to A" /> at once), conserves momentum to rounding error. This is the FMM of falcON and GADGET-4. In practice the tree is built from Morton (Z-order) keys, which sort the particles along a space-filling curve so that every cell is a contiguous range of the sorted array; Lecture 10 introduces them, and they would replace the <M t="(i_x,i_y)" /> sort of the leaf-sorting listing. We do not implement the dual walk here; the uniform quadtree contains all the essential ingredients (P2M, M2M, M2L, L2L, L2P and the near-field sum).

**Periodic boxes.** For cosmological simulations the FMM can be combined with the Ewald sum of Lecture 5: the top-level interactions with all periodic images are replaced by an Ewald-type correction to the local expansion of the root.

## Summary

- The FMM evaluates the field of far cells once per target *cell* (a local expansion) rather than once per target particle, and translates expansions exactly between levels: <M t="O(N)" /> operations for a fixed accuracy.
- In 2D with complex numbers: multipole coefficients <M t="c_k=\sum q(z_j-z_c)^k" />; M2M and L2L are binomial shifts (the parallel-axis theorem generalised); M2L is a <M t="p\times p" /> matrix that depends only on the centre offset.
- The interaction list (children of the parent's neighbours that are not neighbours) has <M t="27" /> boxes in 2D and makes every pair of particles appear exactly once, in the near field or in one list.
- The error falls geometrically, at most <M t="0.55^p" /> (observed <M t="\approx0.4^p" />) for the closest allowed boxes, so <M t="p\approx8" /> gives <M t="10^{-6}" /> (typical) with a uniform tree; the near-field and far-field costs balance at <M t="\sim10" />&ndash;<M t="50" /> particles per leaf.
- Uniform trees suffer for clustered particles; astrophysical FMMs use adaptive trees, dual-tree walks, low orders and softened near fields.
