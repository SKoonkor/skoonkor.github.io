## Beyond the point mass

In Lecture 4 a distant cell acted as a single point mass at its centre of mass. That is the first term of an expansion. If we keep the next non-vanishing term we can use *larger* <M t="\theta" /> (fewer interactions) at the same accuracy, or the same <M t="\theta" /> at much higher accuracy. This lecture derives that term, adds it to your tree, and then compares different rules for deciding when a cell is "far enough". Finally it treats the periodic box that cosmological simulations use.

## The multipole expansion

Let a group of particles <M t="j" /> (masses <M t="m_j" />, positions <M t="\vect r_j" />) have total mass <M t="M" /> and centre of mass <M t="\vect R" />, and write <M t="\vect u_j=\vect r_j-\vect R" /> for the offsets (*not* to be confused with the cell side <M t="s" /> of Lecture 4, which returns in the section on opening criteria), so that <M t="\sum_jm_j\vect u_j=\vect 0" />. A field point <M t="\vect x" /> far from the group has <M t="\vect d=\vect x-\vect R" />, <M t="d=|\vect d|\gg|\vect u_j|" />.

The expansion is a Taylor series in the small ratio <M t="u/d" />: write <M t="|\vect d-\vect u|^{-1}=d^{-1}\big(1-2\,\vect u\!\cdot\!\vect d/d^2+u^2/d^2\big)^{-1/2}" /> and expand <M t="(1+x)^{-1/2}=1-\tfrac12x+\tfrac38x^2-\dots" /> to second order (or use Legendre polynomials). The result, for each particle, is

<M d tag="1" id="eq-l05-expansion" t="\frac{1}{|\vect d-\vect u|}=\frac1d+\frac{\vect u\cdot\vect d}{d^3}+\frac{3(\vect u\cdot\vect d)^2-u^2d^2}{2d^5}+\Oh{\frac{u^3}{d^4}} ." />

Multiplying by <M t="-Gm_j" /> and summing over <M t="j" />:

<M d tag="2" id="eq-l05-phi" t="\Phi(\vect x)=-G\left[\frac{M}{d}+\frac{\vect d\cdot\sum_jm_j\vect u_j}{d^3}+\frac{1}{2d^5}\sum_jm_j\big(3(\vect u_j\cdot\vect d)^2-u_j^2d^2\big)+\dots\right]." />

The three terms are the **monopole**, **dipole** and **quadrupole** (names for the orders <M t="u^0" />, <M t="u^1" />, <M t="u^2" /> of the expansion in <M t="u/d" />; the next one, <M t="u^3" />, is the **octupole**; together they are called *multipoles*). The dipole is <M t="\sum_jm_j\vect u_j=\vect0" /> *because <M t="\vect R" /> is the centre of mass*, so it vanishes. The quadrupole term can be written with the **second-moment tensor**

<M d tag="3" id="eq-l05-I" t="I_{ab}=\sum_jm_j\,u_{j,a}\,u_{j,b},\qquad \mathrm{tr}\,I=\sum_jm_ju_j^2," />

a symmetric <M t="3\times3" /> matrix: <M t="\sum_jm_j(3(\vect u_j\cdot\vect d)^2-u_j^2d^2)=3\,\vect d\!\cdot\! I\!\cdot\!\vect d-\mathrm{tr}I\,d^2" />. (The combination <M t="Q=3I-\mathrm{tr}I\,\mathbb 1" /> is the *traceless* quadrupole tensor often quoted in textbooks; the plain second moment <M t="I" /> carries the same information and is easier to add up.) So

<M d tag="4" id="eq-l05-phiquad" t="\Phi(\vect x)\approx-G\left[\frac{M}{d}+\frac{3\,\vect d\cdot I\cdot\vect d-\mathrm{tr}I\;d^2}{2d^5}\right]." />

```python title="s1_second_moment.py"
import numpy as np

m = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 1.0])                       # a small group (3D)
x = np.array([[0., 0, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0]])
M = m.sum()
com = (m[:, None] * x).sum(axis=0) / M              # centre of mass
s = x - com                                         # offsets from the centre of mass
I = (m[:, None, None] * s[:, :, None] * s[:, None, :]).sum(axis=0)   # sum m s s^T
print(np.round(com, 3))                             # [0.5  0.25 0.  ]
print(np.round(I, 4), np.trace(I))                  # symmetric, trace 1.75
```

**The acceleration.** With <M t="\vect d_c=\vect R-\vect r_i" /> pointing from the particle <M t="i" /> to the group (the convention of Lecture 4), and including Plummer softening by replacing <M t="d^2\to D^2=d^2+\epsilon^2" /> in the denominators (this is exactly the expansion of the softened kernel <M t="1/\sqrt{|\vect d-\vect u|^2+\epsilon^2}" />), differentiating gives (derivation below)

<M d tag="5" id="eq-l05-quadacc" t="\vect a=G\left[\frac{M\,\vect d_c}{D^3}-\frac{3\,\mathrm{tr}I\;\vect d_c+6\,I\vect d_c}{2D^5}+\frac{15\,(\vect d_c\cdot I\cdot\vect d_c)\,\vect d_c}{2D^7}\right]." />

The first term is the monopole force of Lecture 4. The other two are the quadrupole correction.

**Where Eq. (5) comes from.** Start from the softened potential of the group, obtained exactly as above but with the kernel <M t="-1/\sqrt{|\vect d-\vect u|^2+\epsilon^2}" />; expanding to second order in <M t="\vect u" /> gives the same terms with <M t="d^2" /> replaced by <M t="D^2=d^2+\epsilon^2" /> in the denominators:

<M d t="\Phi=-G\left[\frac MD+\frac{3\,\vect d\cdot I\cdot\vect d}{2D^5}-\frac{\mathrm{tr}I}{2D^3}\right]." />

Here <M t="\vect d=\vect x-\vect R" /> points from the group to the field point. The acceleration is <M t="\vect a=-\nabla_{\vect x}\Phi" />, using <M t="\nabla D^{-n}=-n\,\vect d\,D^{-n-2}" />, <M t="\nabla(\vect d\cdot I\cdot\vect d)=2I\vect d" />, and the fact that <M t="\mathrm{tr}I" /> does not depend on <M t="\vect x" />. Term by term (with the overall sign of <M t="\Phi" /> included):

<M d t="\text{monopole: }-\frac{GM\,\vect d}{D^3},\qquad \text{$I\vect d$ term: }+\frac{3G\,I\vect d}{D^5}-\frac{15G\,(\vect d\!\cdot\! I\!\cdot\!\vect d)\,\vect d}{2D^7},\qquad \text{trace term: }+\frac{3G\,\mathrm{tr}I\,\vect d}{2D^5}." />

Collect them (the trace term has the opposite sign to the <M t="I\vect d" /> term because <M t="\Phi" /> contains <M t="-\mathrm{tr}I/2D^3" />) and switch to <M t="\vect d_c=-\vect d" />, the vector from the particle to the group (only the terms odd in <M t="\vect d" /> change sign): this is Eq. (5). The sign flip from <M t="\vect d" /> to <M t="\vect d_c" /> is the classic place to make a mistake: after implementing it, check that the force of a single far-away group points *towards* the group.

**A worked example (softened).** Two unit masses at <M t="x=\pm0.5" /> on the <M t="x" /> axis: <M t="M=2" />, <M t="\vect R=0" />, <M t="I=\mathrm{diag}(0.5,0,0)" />, <M t="\mathrm{tr}I=0.5" />. Field point <M t="(3,0,0)" />, <M t="\epsilon=0.3" />, <M t="G=1" />, so <M t="\vect d_c=(-3,0,0)" /> and <M t="D^2=9.09" />. Direct sum of the two softened pulls: <M t="a_x=-0.23735" />. Monopole only: <M t="-0.21893" /> (error <M t="1.8\times10^{-2}" />). With the quadrupole terms of Eq. (5): <M t="-0.23655" /> (error <M t="8.0\times10^{-4}" />, <M t="23\times" /> smaller). Use this as a first check of your own `quad_acceleration`.

A useful habit when you derive or implement such formulas: *test them against a direct sum*.

```python title="s3_far_field_check.py"
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(0, 0.2, (6, 3))                      # a group of six particles ...
m = rng.random(6) + 0.5
M, com = m.sum(), (m[:, None] * x).sum(axis=0) / m.sum()

def direct(p):                                      # exact pull of the group on a point p
    d = x - p
    return (m[:, None] * d / (d**2).sum(axis=1)[:, None] ** 1.5).sum(axis=0)

def monopole(p):                                    # the whole group as one mass at its COM
    d = com - p
    return M * d / (d @ d) ** 1.5

for dist in (2.0, 4.0, 8.0):
    p = com + np.array([dist, 0.0, 0.0])
    print(dist, np.linalg.norm(monopole(p) - direct(p)))   # ~16x per doubling
```

<Callout kind="idea">
Each extra multipole order buys one more power of <M t="s/d" /> in accuracy. The monopole force error is relative <M t="\Oh{(s/d)^2}" /> (the quadrupole), so absolute <M t="d^{-4}" />; keeping the quadrupole leaves relative <M t="\Oh{(s/d)^3}" />, absolute <M t="d^{-5}" />. In a tree, <M t="s/d<\theta" />, so the error of a quadrupole interaction is smaller than that of a monopole interaction by a factor <M t="\sim s/d\lesssim\theta" />. The measured gain in the total force is larger (about <M t="4\times" /> at <M t="\theta=0.5" />, with the error falling like <M t="\theta^4" /> against <M t="\theta^{2.6}" />); a plausible interpretation is that the errors of the many cells contributing to a force have partly random signs and partly cancel, which we did not test separately.
</Callout>

## Quadrupoles for every cell: the parallel-axis theorem

Each tree cell needs its tensor <M t="I" />. For a leaf, sum over its particles, Eq. (3). For an internal cell, do *not* loop over all its particles again: build it from its children. Let child <M t="c" /> have mass <M t="M_c" />, centre of mass <M t="\vect R_c" /> and tensor <M t="I_c" /> (about <M t="\vect R_c" />), and let <M t="\vect\delta_c=\vect R_c-\vect R" /> be its offset from the parent's centre of mass. A particle of child <M t="c" /> has offset <M t="\vect u=\vect u'+\vect\delta_c" /> from the parent's centre of mass, where <M t="\vect u'" /> is its offset from <M t="\vect R_c" />. Then <M t="\sum_{j\in c}m_ju_au_b=I_{c,ab}+M_c\delta_{c,a}\delta_{c,b}" /> because the cross terms contain <M t="\sum_{j\in c}m_j\vect u'_j=\vect 0" />. Summing over children:

<M d tag="6" id="eq-l05-parallel" t="I_{ab}=\sum_c\Big(I_{c,ab}+M_c\,\delta_{c,a}\delta_{c,b}\Big),\qquad\vect\delta_c=\vect R_c-\vect R ." />

This is the **parallel-axis theorem**, and it costs <M t="\Oh{1}" /> per child, so the whole tree takes <M t="\Oh{N}" /> extra work, done bottom-up (post-order: children first).

**A worked example (one dimension).** Child A: unit masses at <M t="x=\pm0.5" />, so <M t="M_A=2" />, <M t="R_A=0" />, <M t="I_A=\sum m(x-R_A)^2=0.5" />. Child B: masses <M t="1" /> at <M t="x=3" /> and <M t="2" /> at <M t="x=6" />, so <M t="M_B=3" />, <M t="R_B=(3+12)/3=5" />, <M t="I_B=1\cdot(3-5)^2+2\cdot(6-5)^2=6" />. Parent: <M t="M=5" />, <M t="R=(2\cdot0+3\cdot5)/5=3" />, so <M t="\delta_A=-3" />, <M t="\delta_B=+2" />. Eq. (6):

<M d t="I=\underbrace{(0.5+2\cdot9)}_{A}+\underbrace{(6+3\cdot4)}_{B}=36.5 ." />

Directly, from the four particles about <M t="R=3" /> (offsets <M t="-3.5,-2.5,0,3" />): <M t="I=12.25+6.25+0+2\cdot9=36.5" />. &#10003; Shifting the expansion centre of a group from a child's centre of mass to the parent's is called an **M2M translation** (multipole to multipole); the Fast Multipole Method of Lecture 9 generalises exactly this step to all orders, and adds a translation from a group's multipole to a *local* expansion at the target.

```python title="s2_parallel_axis.py"
import numpy as np

def moments(m, x):
    """Mass, centre of mass and second-moment tensor of a group."""
    M = m.sum()
    com = (m[:, None] * x).sum(axis=0) / M
    s = x - com
    return M, com, (m[:, None, None] * s[:, :, None] * s[:, None, :]).sum(axis=0)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
m, x = rng.random(6) + 0.5, rng.random((6, 3))
A, B = moments(m[:3], x[:3]), moments(m[3:], x[3:])          # two "children"
M, com, I = moments(m, x)                                    # the "parent", directly
I_from_children = sum(I_c + M_c * np.outer(c - com, c - com) for M_c, c, I_c in (A, B))
print(np.abs(I - I_from_children).max() < 1e-12)             # True: parallel-axis theorem
```

## The tree walk with quadrupoles

Nothing changes in the walk except what happens when a cell is accepted: instead of the point-mass force, use Eq. (5). The number of interactions is identical (same criterion, same tree), but each cell interaction costs a few more arithmetic operations. Store <M t="I" /> in the cell record (the `quad` field prepared in Lecture 4), computed once per tree build. Softening enters through <M t="D^2=d^2+\epsilon^2" /> in every term. In your walk the accepted branch becomes: choose the criterion (`accept_geometric`, `accept_offset` or `accept_relative`, the last one using the magnitude `acc_old[i]` of the particle's previous acceleration); if a cell that does not contain the particle is accepted, add `quad_acceleration(d, node.mass, node.quad, G, eps)` if `use_quad`, else the monopole force of Lecture 4 (your walk already calls a separate acceptance function: the Lecture 4 hook).

At <M t="\theta=0.5" /> the quadrupole tree is <M t="4\times" /> more accurate in the median and <M t="6\times" /> in the tail for the *same* number of interactions. **What does it cost?** In pure Python the quadrupole interaction is more expensive: our <M t="N=1000" />, <M t="\theta=0.5" /> walk takes about <M t="1.7\times" /> longer than the monopole walk (typically <M t="2.7" /> s against <M t="1.6" /> s), so at fixed accuracy Python gains little. In compiled code the extra arithmetic is small compared with the memory access needed to fetch the cell, so quadrupoles are usually a win there. Many tree codes use them (Hernquist 1987, PKDGRAV, GADGET-4); GADGET-2 itself uses monopoles only, and relies on a better opening criterion (below) instead. Homework 5.6 asks you to measure this.

## Opening criteria

So far "far enough" meant the geometric rule <M t="s/d<\theta" />. It has two weaknesses: it treats all cells at the same <M t="s/d" /> alike whatever the force scale, and it ignores where the centre of mass sits inside the cell. Three criteria are in common use. Write <M t="s" /> for the cell's side, <M t="d" /> for the distance from the particle to the cell's centre of mass, <M t="\delta=|\vect R-\vect c|" /> for the offset of the centre of mass from the geometric centre <M t="\vect c" /> of the cell.

1. **Geometric** (Barnes&ndash;Hut): accept if <M t="s/d<\theta" />.
2. **Offset** (a common refinement; see also the error-bounded criteria of Salmon & Warren 1994): accept if <M t="d>s/\theta+\delta" />. If the centre of mass sits on the *far* side of the cell (as seen from the particle), the body of the cell extends towards the particle and its nearest particles are closer than <M t="d" /> suggests; the offset criterion is stricter for such cells. It is a uniform conservative margin: it subtracts <M t="\delta" /> whatever the direction.
3. **Relative** (GADGET-2, Springel 2005): accept if <M t="\displaystyle\frac{GM\,s^2}{d^4}\le\alpha\,|\vect a_{\rm old}|" />, where <M t="\vect a_{\rm old}" /> is the particle's acceleration from the previous step (or from a first cheap pass). The left side estimates the size of the neglected quadrupole force, <M t="\frac{GM}{d^2}\big(\frac sd\big)^2" />; the criterion demands that it be a fraction <M t="\alpha" /> of the particle's own total acceleration. Typical <M t="\alpha=0.005" />.

**Why the relative criterion?** A fixed angle <M t="\theta" /> treats every particle alike. But a given absolute force error matters little to a particle that feels a strong force (large <M t="|\vect a|" />) and a lot to one that feels a weak force (small <M t="|\vect a|" />, for example near the centre of a cluster where the pulls almost cancel, or far outside it). The relative criterion asks instead for the same tolerance <M t="\alpha" /> of *the particle's own acceleration*. It uses the previous force calculation: after each step you store <M t="|\vect a_i|" /> for every particle and use it for the next step (on the first step, compute it once with a cheap geometric pass). A slightly stale <M t="\vect a_{\rm old}" /> is harmless, since it only sets a tolerance; if <M t="|\vect a_{\rm old}|" /> is tiny the criterion becomes strict and opens many cells, which is the safe direction. The estimate <M t="GMs^2/d^4" /> is the size of the neglected quadrupole term of a monopole tree; with quadrupoles the neglected term is the octupole, so the same criterion is conservative there.

```python title="s4_criteria_toy.py"
import numpy as np

half, com, center = 0.5, np.array([2.25, 0.0]), np.array([1.95, 0.0])   # side 1
p = np.zeros(2)                                     # the particle
theta, alpha, G, M, a_old = 0.5, 0.005, 1.0, 2.0, 1.0
d = np.linalg.norm(com - p)                         # distance to the COM = 2.25
s = 2 * half                                        # cell side
delta = np.linalg.norm(com - center)                # COM offset from the cell centre
print(s / d < theta)                                # geometric: 0.44 < 0.5 True
print(d > s / theta + delta)                        # offset: 2.25 > 2.3 False
print(G * M * s**2 <= alpha * a_old * d**4)         # relative: 2 <= 0.13 False
```

Keep the rule that a cell containing the particle is never accepted, whatever the criterion. With the offset criterion be careful at <M t="\theta=0" />: <M t="s/\theta" /> diverges, and the correct answer is "never accept". For the relative criterion you need <M t="|\vect a_{\rm old}|" />: on the first step there is none, so start from a cheap geometric pass (or fall back to the geometric criterion), and keep the acceleration of the previous step afterwards.
</Callout>

Figure 6 is the honest summary. On this smooth cluster: quadrupoles help a lot; the offset criterion is not better than the geometric one at equal cost (its purpose is to avoid rare bad cases, at some cost); the relative criterion gives <M t="\sim4" />&ndash;<M t="6\times" /> smaller maximum error (a single worst particle, so it varies from sample to sample) at <M t="\sim290" /> interactions per particle than the geometric criterion at the same cost. On clustered distributions (two Gaussian clumps in a sparse background, <M t="\epsilon=0.01" />) the relative criterion again gives a smaller median error at moderate cost (typically a factor <M t="2" />&ndash;<M t="3" />), but its tail is less controlled when <M t="\alpha" /> is loose (at <M t="\alpha=0.02" /> we saw individual errors of tens of percent). So **no criterion wins everywhere**: the ranking depends on the distribution and on which error, median or worst case, you care about. The relative criterion is popular in cosmology because it targets what dynamics needs, an error bounded relative to the particle's own acceleration, rather than a fixed opening angle.

**Tree walks with a cutoff.** Lecture 8 needs a tree that only computes the *short-range* part of the force, with a maximum interaction distance <M t="r_{\rm cut}" />. The walk then skips every cell whose nearest point to the particle is farther than <M t="r_{\rm cut}" /> (measured to the cell's box, not to its centre of mass). Nothing else changes.

## Periodic boundaries and the Ewald sum

Cosmological simulations model a small, representative cube of the Universe with **periodic boundary conditions**: a particle leaving one face re-enters at the opposite face, and every particle also feels all the periodic copies (images) of every other one. The force on a particle is then a sum over an infinite lattice of images, <M t="\sum_{\vect n\in\mathbb Z^3}m_j(\vect r_j-\vect r_i+\vect nL)/|\dots|^3" />. Two problems arise. Keeping only the nearest image (the *minimum-image* convention) is a poor approximation: compared with the full periodic force (Ewald, below; measured with the reference solution) the nearest-image Newtonian force is off by about <M t="0.4\%" /> at a separation <M t="r\approx0.1L" />, <M t="10" />&ndash;<M t="17\%" /> (depending on direction) at <M t="r\approx0.3L" />, and by tens of percent or more near the edge of the box. At small <M t="r" /> the difference is almost entirely the uniform background that periodic simulations subtract. And the full sum converges only conditionally (its value depends on the order of summation), because <M t="1/r^2" /> decays too slowly compared with the growth of the number of images.

**The Ewald method (1921).** Here <M t="\mathrm{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^xe^{-t^2}dt" /> is the error function and <M t="\mathrm{erfc}=1-\mathrm{erf}" />. Split the kernel into a short-range and a smooth part,

<M d tag="7" id="eq-l05-split" t="\frac1r=\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(\alpha r)}{r}}_{\text{short range}}+\underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{erf}(\alpha r)}{r}}_{\text{smooth}} ." />

The first part decays like a Gaussian, so only the few nearest images matter (*real-space* sum). The second is the potential of a smooth (Gaussian-blurred) mass distribution, so its Fourier series over the periodic box, with wave vectors <M t="\vect k=2\pi\vect m/L" /> (<M t="\vect m" /> a triple of integers), converges after a few low modes (*reciprocal-space* sum; the Poisson equation is algebraic in Fourier space, which is why this works). The conditional convergence of the naive image sum is an artefact of summing shells in a fixed order; the periodic Universe has zero mean density, which the background term restores. You do not derive the result below: you only evaluate it in Homework 5.7. Adding a uniform negative background so that the total density has zero mean (this removes the divergent <M t="\vect k=0" /> term) makes the sum well defined, and the potential of a unit mass at the origin of a cube of side <M t="L" /> is

<M d tag="8" id="eq-l05-ewald" t="\phi(\vect r)=\sum_{\vect n}\frac{\mathrm{erfc}(\alpha|\vect r+\vect nL|)}{|\vect r+\vect nL|}+\frac{1}{\pi L}\sum_{\vect m\ne\vect0}\frac{e^{-\pi^2m^2/(\alpha L)^2}}{m^2}\cos\frac{2\pi\,\vect m\cdot\vect r}{L}-\frac{\pi}{\alpha^2L^3}," />

with the gravitational potential energy <M t="-Gm\,\phi" /> and acceleration <M t="\vect a=G\,m\,\nabla\phi" />. The parameter <M t="\alpha" /> is arbitrary: the *result* does not depend on it, only the convergence speed does. A good choice is <M t="\alpha\approx2/L" />, for which two image shells and <M t="|\vect m|\le5" /> give an accuracy of about <M t="10^{-12}" />. Near the origin, <M t="\phi\to1/r-2.8373/L" />: the images and the background add a constant.

```python title="s5_ewald_split.py"
from math import erf, erfc

alpha, r = 2.0, 0.37
short = erfc(alpha * r) / r          # decays like a Gaussian: needs only nearby images
smooth = erf(alpha * r) / r          # smooth everywhere: easy in Fourier space
print(short + smooth - 1 / r)        # 0.0: the two parts add up to the Newtonian 1/r
```

Evaluating Eq. (8) for every pair is far too slow (about <M t="10^{-4}" /> s per pair in Python). Production tree codes therefore *tabulate* the Ewald correction (the difference between the periodic force and the Newtonian force of the nearest image) once on a grid over half of the box (Hernquist, Bouchet & Suto 1991), and interpolate. The table covers one octant of the box (coordinates from <M t="0" /> to <M t="L/2" />), because the force has the symmetries of the cube. The tree walk itself needs only a small change: use the nearest image of each cell and add the tabulated correction. Fourier-based solvers (Lecture 6) get periodicity for free: a periodic particle&ndash;mesh solver is essentially the reciprocal-space sum of the Ewald method over the grid's modes, with no image sum. In Lecture 8 the same erfc split reappears as a Gaussian split of the force, <M t="\mathrm{erfc}(r/2r_s)" /> for the short-range (tree) part, that is <M t="\alpha=1/(2r_s)" />, with the mesh handling the smooth part (Fourier factor <M t="e^{-k^2r_s^2}" />).

## Summary

- About the centre of mass the dipole vanishes; the next term is the quadrupole, described by the second-moment tensor <M t="I=\sum m\,\vect u\vect u^{\rm T}" />. It gives an extra power of <M t="s/d" /> in accuracy (absolute error <M t="d^{-5}" /> instead of <M t="d^{-4}" />).
- <M t="I" /> of every cell is built bottom-up with the parallel-axis theorem in <M t="\Oh{N}" /> total work. Softening enters through <M t="D^2=d^2+\epsilon^2" />.
- Quadrupoles make the tree error fall like <M t="\theta^4" />; at <M t="\theta=0.5" /> they cut the median error by <M t="\sim4\times" /> at equal interaction count.
- Opening criteria trade differently on different distributions; the relative (force-scale) criterion of GADGET-2 targets an error that is a fixed fraction of the particle's own acceleration.
- Periodic boxes need the Ewald sum: split <M t="1/r" /> into a short-range real-space part and a smooth Fourier part; the result is independent of the splitting parameter.
