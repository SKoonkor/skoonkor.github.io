Why this matters

A galaxy is millions of these batches of stars added together, and a simulated universe holds millions of galaxies. Working out the light of every one of them, wavelength by wavelength, is the step that makes the calculation unaffordable.

Keeping 87 numbers instead of 1200 reproduces the spectrum to better than 1 per cent — about 14× less to compute and store. That is the difference between a calculation you can run across a whole model universe and one you cannot.

The detail

Principal component analysis is applied to 106 simple stellar population spectra from FSPS with a Kroupa (2001) IMF, at solar metallicity, spanning 0.1 Myr to 20 Gyr. Each spectrum is reconstructed as f(λ) = η [ x̄(λ) + Σj αjvj(λ) ].

The analysis runs separately on three wavelength bands. A single whole-range basis reproduces the optical almost perfectly but cannot reach the far ultraviolet, where an old population emits about 10−4 of its peak flux. The residual panel greys out the region below the Lyman break, where there is so little light that a percentage error stops meaning anything.

Wavelength grid 1200 log-spaced bins over 1000–30 000 Å, decimated from the FSPS native 4842
Normalisation L2 norm, on linear flux. A logarithmic normalisation reconstructs better, and the thesis shows this, then rejects it: a composite stellar population is a linear superposition of these spectra, so a log-space basis cannot be summed and the compression buys nothing in the galaxy formation model it exists to serve.

Method and results follow the MSc thesis, Application of Principal Component Analysis to Galaxy Spectral Energy Distributions (Durham, 2020). The basis here is regenerated from scratch rather than taken from the original analysis, so the component counts differ slightly.
